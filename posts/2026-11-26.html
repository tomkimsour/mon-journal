Robot evaluation is moving past one success number: judges are being checked against humans and kept outside the training loop, policies are tested on people changing their minds, and embodied agents are scored on whether they waited for enough evidence before acting.

Today's papers keep finding that the usual success numbers hide where robots actually fail. Simulator predicates and in-loop judges disagree with people, and the fixes are human-aligned or sealed evaluators that change policy rankings. VLAs that score above 95% on fixed instructions almost never follow a changed instruction mid-task, which is exactly what a person in the room will do. Perception and world models fail in ways their own confidence hides: SLAM that is fully confident in the dark, depth that helps until holes appear, world models trained only on successful grasps. On the social side, new benchmarks score timing and sufficiency of evidence (when to respond, when to hold back, when a memory is stale) rather than recognition alone. For a team building robots that live with people of different ages and cultures, the practical move is to test the moments where a human changes the situation and to treat 'not yet sure' as a correct output.

Insights

  1. Mid-task human input is the cheapest robustness test you are not running. DTU-Bench and the instruction black hole paper show near-total failure to follow a changed instruction, and both fixes work by moving the robot back to an approach state rather than retraining. For HRI work, script a few 'actually, take the other one' interruptions into every evaluation, and measure both whether the robot switches and whether it disturbs objects on the way, which the scripted retreat sometimes does.
  2. Several frozen VLAs got large gains purely from test-time intervention. Cairn steers a frozen π0.5 along object trajectories from 24.5% to 95.1% on displaced-object LIBERO scenes, ahead of a fine-tuned π0.5. Removing the temporal mean of the initial noise lifts real-robot π0.5 from 40% to 68%, and optimizing the sampling schedule adds 2.8 points. Before fine-tuning a VLA on a new layout, try steering the sampler or the trajectory and keep the weights you know.
  3. Treat abstention as a feature to train and test, not a failure. HumblePRM increases abstention on ambiguous images while leaving clear-image accuracy roughly unchanged, EvOrderBench shows open models commit to an event order even when the frames do not establish it, and AIRIS penalizes guessing intent too early. For robots that serve children, older adults or people with unfamiliar norms, an agent that waits or asks is safer than one that guesses; make that a measured outcome in user studies.
  4. Count independent evidence, not repeated evidence. The Spot memory study finds nearby revisits often reproduce the same VLM miss, and SealedLoop shows an in-loop judge shares errors with the policy it scores. In both cases the remedy is to discount correlated signals and add a source that the system under test cannot influence.

  • Effect-Driven Skill Abstractions for Offline Reinforcement Learning RO
    Burcu Kilic, David Drexel, Emre Ugur, Justus Piater · arXiv:2611.33229
    Abstract

    We seek to learn discrete skill abstractions to facilitate long-horizon, goal-conditioned reinforcement-learning (RL) tasks. Recent approaches have shown success by quantizing actions via clustering or reconstruction. However, these objectives lead to skills that are not directly related to what the current state affords or what the goal requires. Instead, we argue that goal-directed skills should be driven by \emph{how they affect the state} of the agent.   • DexEngine: Scaling Human Demonstrations into Physically Executable Whole-Robot Data RO/AI
    Wongyun Yu, Honggeun Kim, Donguk Lee, Minchan Kim et al. · arXiv:2611.33579
    Abstract

    A growing body of recent work addresses the retargeting of human hand-object demonstrations to robotic hands. To train whole-robot policies, however, the retargeted motions must be kinematically feasible and physically executable, and be produced efficiently at scale. We present DexEngine, a simulation pipeline that turns human manipulation data into train-ready, physically executable arm-hand trajectories. DexEngine first places each clip's hand-object motion where the arms can reach it, finds an arm motion that follows the human hands, and then corrects the motion in physics simulation with residual reinforcement learning. From nine human hand-object datasets, we generate about 5,200 clips for each of four dexterous hands, augmented into 65,000 trajectories (116 h) and 325,000 episodes (578 h) in total. Even with arms attached, DexEngine tracks objects more accurately than prior floating-hand methods and runs over 10x faster than RL-based ones. Policies trained on our data far outperform those trained on the same clips without physics correction or with the arms attached after a floating-hand correction, showing that fine-tuning data need physics correction. Mid-training on our data further improves them at equal fine-tuning budget, in simulation and on a real robot.

  • $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM: Efficient Hierarchical Memory Across Modalities and Timescales for World-Action Models RO
    Xiaopeng Zhang, Weiqi Yuan, Xin Zhu, Jiaxi Huang et al. · arXiv:2611.33710
    Abstract

    Long-horizon robotic manipulation requires historical context, yet retaining extensive visual histories incurs growing storage and retrieval costs, while aggressive compression risks losing fine-grained information needed for subsequent decisions. Moreover, executed action histories capture intermediate behaviors that sparse visual observations may miss. Preserving this complementary context efficiently calls for modality-specific memory mechanisms that balance recent detail with long-term retention. To this end, we introduce $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM, a world-action model equipped with hierarchical visual and interaction memories. $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM preserves recent visual observations in dense short-term memory while consolidating entries evicted from this window into fixed-size associative long-term memory through gated delta updates.   • A general-purpose safe manipulator planning framework for diverse environments without retraining RO
    Mingkang Long, Rong Su, Luhang Hong, Qingqing Wang et al. · arXiv:2611.33721
    Abstract

    Nowadays, robots need to be retrained whenever their operating environment changes, and this prohibitive training cost keeps them far from daily use. The community is calling for innovations that bestow on a robot the ability to safely grasp different objects under different conditions without excessive training data and computing power. In response to this call, we present a general-purpose manipulator mission-planning framework, allowing a robot to safely perform manipulation tasks, such as grasping objects that fit in its gripper, across different operating environments without retraining its controllers.   • Targeted Adaptation of World-Action Models via Interaction and Motion Heads RO/CV

    Abstract

  • QK-Bench: Benchmarking Quantitative Kinematic Understanding for Dynamic Spatial Intelligence RO/CV
    Lennox Wei, Jiaqi Yao, Shixin Sun, Yuanzheng Zhang et al. · arXiv:2611.33991
    Abstract

    Spatial intelligence concerns both scene structure and its temporal evolution. Yet a semantically correct description can conceal a quantitative error: recognizing that objects are approaching does not determine their closing speed or whether they will meet. Such errors are critical in collision avoidance, trajectory tracking, and reactive grasping, where actions depend on motion magnitude, timing, and relative geometry. We study quantitative kinematic understanding in multimodal large language models (MLLMs): resolving motion magnitude, rate, reference-frame dependence, and future spatial evolution from visual evidence.
