Robots trained with one partner, one sensor condition or one demo set break on the next one, and today's fixes randomize the hidden variable, gate unreliable perception, and mine the policy's own failures.

The common thread today is what happens when a robot meets something its training did not show it. A self-play handover policy that succeeds 95% of the time with its own partner drops to 31% with unfamiliar ones, and an accessibility benchmark shows VLMs failing to change their path choices across user profiles. On the perception side, navigation and locomotion papers stop assuming the depth or image is right and build explicit fallback, revision and re-check steps. On the learning side, several groups generate post-training data from the states where a VLA or reasoning model fails, mostly in simulation, while controlled single-factor studies (perception noise, cross-view geometry, contact cues) explain why policies are brittle in the first place. For a team building robots that interact with people of different ages and cultures, the practical message is to treat the human partner as a distribution to randomize and test against, the same way perception noise already is.

Insights

  1. Randomize the human, not only the scene. The handover paper shows a policy can look fluent while having learned a single rendezvous time, and a one-line rule that hides a random start delay from the receiver raises success with unseen partners from 31% to 80%. VLAN and the Talk to Act dataset push the same idea into navigation and dialogue through user profiles and personas. Before running a user study, audit which partner timings, mobility needs and phrasing styles your training ever covered.
  2. Make degraded perception a mode with its own behavior. PROOF, ERA and the relighting work each define what the robot does when perception is unreliable: fall back to proprioception, keep moving to collect views that fix the map, or invoke an expensive model only on a confidence drop. ERA's map and exploration components raise VLFM's success from 10.8% to 61.7% under the same predicted depth, which suggests much of the gap is in how errors are handled, not in the depth itself.
  3. Close-loop tests are not optional for perception changes. The proprioceptive-anchor study finds that the discount a policy applies to noisy readings in training does not carry over to deployment, where closed-loop sensitivity to an offset exceeded the predicted value in all 15 policies that passed its gates. Offline robustness metrics on perception noise should be treated as a lower bound on how much the robot will actually follow a bad reading.
  4. An open-weight reasoning model driving a real arm without a harness is now a credible baseline. PEAR reports 60% success on a real dual-arm robot from sim-only RL and 57.4% on LIBERO-Pro new tasks where a fine-tuned π0.5 gets 0.75%. Teams evaluating VLAs for instruction variety should add such a robot-use agent as a comparison point, while checking latency on their own hardware since the abstract does not report it.

When Will You Hand It Over? A Hidden Start Delay Makes Self-Play Handover Robust to Unfamiliar Partners RO
Mohammad Khoshnazar, Mike Degany, Deyuan Qu, Qing Yang et al. · arXiv:2611.18491
Why read it
Handover is one of the most common physical human-robot interactions, and this paper shows exactly how a policy that looks fluent fails with a new partner.
Key idea
Under shared-reward self-play the learned giver always starts its offer immediately, so the receiver learns one rendezvous time; hiding a random start delay on the training partner forces the receiver to wait and react.
Evidence
Physics-based parcel handover with a Unitree G1 receiver: 95% and 90% success with its own partner across two seeds, 31% with unseen scripted partners; a one-second delay drops success from 95% to 6%; after 300 updates with hidden start-delay randomisation, unseen-partner success rises to 80% in both seeds against 35–41% for a budget-matched control, holds on eleven held-out partner conditions, and cuts high-force contact three- to five-fold.
Caveat
All partners are learned or scripted agents in simulation; no human givers or hardware trials are reported, and timing is only one of many ways real people differ.
VLAN: Vision-Language Accessible Navigation AI/CV
Jie Hu, Yu Zheng, Wenzhi Wu, Weixing Feng et al. · arXiv:2611.18346
Why read it
It is one of the few navigation benchmarks that treats who the robot is moving for, including people with visual or mobility impairments, as part of the task.
Key idea
Traversability is user-conditioned: each trajectory carries embodiment-specific actions and obstacles labelled Passable, Bypass, HighRisk or Blocked, and metrics score accessibility alignment and collisions, not just goal reaching.
Evidence
1,000 scenes, 20K trajectories and 160K keyframes covering legged and wheeled robots and people with visual or mobility impairments; benchmarked VLMs often fail to adapt obstacle relevance, path selection and safety criteria across user profiles. Data and code are stated to be open-source.
Caveat
The abstract gives no per-model numbers, and it does not say whether scenes are real captures or synthetic, so sim-to-real relevance for an assistive robot is unclear.
Training Reasoning Models in Simulation to Natively Use Real Robots RO
Jiaming Tang, Zhuoyang Zhang, Junxian Guo, Fangzhou Zhao et al. · arXiv:2611.18562
Why read it
It gives a concrete recipe for getting an open-weight model to control a real robot from sim-only RL, and a striking robustness comparison with a demonstration-trained VLA on new tasks.
Key idea
Treat robot control like computer use: a 27B reasoning model observes, reasons in a chain of thought, and outputs the next end-effector pose, trained with reinforcement learning in simulation and no demonstrations.
Evidence
On LIBERO, success rises from 1.6% to 74.4%; on a real dual-arm robot the trained model succeeds in 60% of trials against none for the base model; on LIBERO-Pro new tasks in a familiar scene it reaches 57.4% where π0.5 reaches 0.75%; no measurable degradation on nine reasoning, coding and vision benchmarks.
Caveat
The abstract does not report inference latency or control rate for a 27B model with chain-of-thought, and the real-robot result is a single platform with an unstated number of tasks.
Imitation Policies Learn a Proprioceptive Anchor: A Predictive Law for Perception Noise and Its Closed Loop Cost RO/AI
Christopher Malili · arXiv:2611.17658
Why read it
Most deployed manipulation policies consume noisy object poses, and this paper gives a formula that predicts how much the policy will trust them, plus a warning that the prediction fails in closed loop.
Key idea
Because demonstrators steer toward the object, the robot's own state is a proprioceptive anchor for object position; the policy weights the noisy reading by α = V/(V + s²), where V is the anchor's error variance as learned by the policy.
Evidence
Measured once, V predicted ten held-out sensitivities across two robomimic tasks, two learners and four noise levels with mean absolute error 0.025 against 0.37 for classical errors-in-variables regression; closed-loop sensitivity exceeded α in all 15 policies that passed the registered gates; tests were registered before data collection.
Caveat
All results are in simulation with synthetic Gaussian noise; real perception errors are biased and correlated, and the author notes training noise predicts success as well as the measured α.
Grounding Object-to-Object Hazard Reasoning into 3D Risk Fields for Task-Conditioned Safety RO
Mariana Sosa Guzmán, Yide Song, Amin Abyaneh, Gregory Dudek et al. · arXiv:2611.17566
Why read it
For robots working around people at home or in care settings, it shows a practical way to turn a VLM's commonsense about hazardous object pairs into a geometric constraint a motion layer can enforce.
Key idea
A VLM first decides whether the task should be refused because of non-recoverable hazards; otherwise it names object-pair relations to avoid, which are grounded with open-vocabulary segmentation and depth into continuous 3D risk fields used by obstacle avoidance.
Evidence
Evaluated on preventable hazards, tasks requiring rejection, and safe variants in simulation and the real world.
Caveat
The abstract reports no success, false-refusal or hazard-avoidance rates, so the trade-off between over-refusing and missing hazards cannot be judged from it.

  • Securing Multi-Agent Systems by Design AI
    Xilong Wang, Xiaoxing Chen, Neil Gong · arXiv:2611.17883
    Abstract

    Multi-agent systems solve complex tasks by decomposing them into subtasks, delegating the subtasks to agents, and aggregating the agents' responses using an aggregation rule. Malicious agents can manipulate the final solution by providing malicious responses to their assigned subtasks. In this paper, we first propose response manipulation attacks (ReMA), which iteratively optimize malicious responses to maximize attack effectiveness. Specifically, we formulate untargeted and targeted attacks as optimization problems and develop an approach to approximately solve these problems by iteratively refining malicious responses based on feedback from a surrogate multi-agent system. To defend against such attacks, we further propose SecMAS, a secure aggregation rule that leverages the semantic similarities among agent responses to select the response that is most consistent with a sufficiently large subset of the other responses. We theoretically establish security guarantees for SecMAS against both untargeted and targeted attacks. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that existing aggregation rules are vulnerable to ReMA, whereas SecMAS effectively defends against all evaluated attacks, including adaptive variants of ReMA, while maintaining utility comparable to that of existing aggregation rules.

  • VACO: Vertex-Aligned Decoding for Dense Hand Contact Estimation from a Single Image CV
    Binhong Ye, Jin-Chuan Shi, Tao Liu, Xin Wang et al. · arXiv:2611.17755
    Abstract

    Dense hand contact estimation from a single RGB image is a foundational primitive for dexterous manipulation and 3D interaction analysis. Direct predictors typically decode the entire per-vertex contact map from one shared query, leaving individual vertices ungrounded in the image and smearing contact across adjacent, non-touching surfaces. We present VACO, a vertex-aligned decoder in which each MANO vertex samples local image features at its projected location.   • DIRT: Depth-completed Image-based Rendering using Two-band Blending for Live Novel-view Synthesis CV
    Mathias Harrer, Laura Fink, Noah Lewis, Paul Himmler et al. · arXiv:2611.18340
    Abstract

    State-of-the-art novel-view synthesis (NVS) methods commonly require per-scene optimization before rendering, limiting their applicability in live settings. Live or ad-hoc NVS, however, demands low-latency rendering during capture and strong generalization to new scenes. Some previous work tried to address this task by adapting training- and optimization-based methods, trading quality for low latency. We introduce a live NVS approach following traditional image-based rendering that operates on a posed set of depth sensor observations and images. Our method leverages explicit, sparse point-based geometry obtained from active sensors, resulting in a sparsely filled depth buffer that is completed in the (novel) target view's screen-space. Using this proxy, a posed set of images is projected into the target frame before fusing the resulting observations through a frequency-aware two-band blending strategy. The pipeline is entirely non-neural and optimization-free, requiring no training data or scene-specific optimization. As a result, our approach reduces the delay between capture and novel-view rendering and does not rely on favorable network generalization. We validate the method's applicability to heterogeneous sensor setups - including handheld RGB-D cameras, automotive LiDAR, and thermal cameras - as well as challenging scenarios, such as low light captures. Our experiments demonstrate that the approach outperforms prior methods in live reconstruction scenarios, while maintaining competitive performance in conventional NVS benchmarks.

  • D2ToF: One-Step Diffusion for Depth Super-Resolution from Lightweight Single-Photon LiDAR Waveforms CV
    Pallabjyoti Deka, Lewis Wilson, Stephen McLaughlin, Jonathan Leach et al. · arXiv:2611.18243
    Abstract

    Single-photon LiDAR (SPL) sensors offer exceptional temporal resolution and single-photon sensitivity for depth imaging. However, cost-effective SPL arrays typically suffer from extremely low spatial resolutions (e.g. $8\times 8$ pixels), limiting their real-world utility. In this paper, we propose a novel, one-step super-resolution framework, D2ToF, Distilled Diffusion for Time-of-Flight, that bridges the gap between high-fidelity generative modeling and real-time execution. Our approach uses an amortized variational distillation strategy that allows for a measurement-based data consistency term to be incorporated directly into the one-step training objective. This encourages that the synthesized high-resolution depth maps are consistent with the captured low-resolution Time-of-Flight (ToF) histograms. To overcome the severe ill-posedness of upsampling extremely degraded inputs, we introduce a multimodal guidance strategy. By leveraging a foundational monocular depth model to extract structural priors from a high-resolution reflectivity image, our network is guided by a rich triad of the ToF histogram, reflectivity map, and relative depth estimate. Experimental results outperform evaluated baselines, achieving $16\times$ spatial super-resolution (from $8\times 8 \times 100$ histograms to $128\times 128$ depth maps) at an accelerated inference speed of approximately $83$ milliseconds.
