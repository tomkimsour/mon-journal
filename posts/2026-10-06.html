Social robots are starting to plan how loud, fast and expressive they are, while new probes show VLAs often ignore what the person actually asked.

Several papers today treat social behaviour as something the robot actively produces: footstep noise, gesture timing, facial expression and execution speed are planned outputs, not side effects. A second group treats the human as a physical partner with their own forces, fatigue and fragility rather than as an obstacle or a remote operator. At the same time, diagnostic work on VLAs shows that nominal task success hides weak instruction following: policies grab the object the scene suggests, and safety refusals learned in text do not carry over to actions or grounding outputs. The practical consequence for an HRI team is that interaction quality has to be measured channel by channel, and that 'does the robot do what this person asked, and refuse what it should' needs its own tests. Much of the remaining engineering work fixes frozen policies at execution time, which is where a deployment team can act today.

Insights

  1. Measure social behaviour per channel, and with diverse people. TACET reports up to 9.3 dBA less locomotion noise at matched speed while keeping 100% personal-space compliance, and ARISE and TRABot coordinate speech, gesture and face in real time; the tour-guide protocol names perceived safety and perceived intelligence as outcome constructs. None of these abstracts mention evaluations across age groups or cultures, which is exactly where norms about loudness, gaze, gesture and approach distance differ. A useful next study: run the same robot behaviour (noise level, gesture set, approach speed) with participant groups of different ages and cultural backgrounds and report each channel's ratings separately.
  2. Instruction following is the HRI failure that standard VLA benchmarks hide. In the grounding-gap study, policies asked to pick a different visible object mostly pick the original one, and in the implied-harm study up to 95% of implied-harm runs finish the task with no guard flag. Add two cheap tests to any manipulation acceptance suite: swap the target object in the instruction while keeping the scene fixed, and phrase the same request with indirect intent. Track 'followed the changed request' and 'refused when it should' as separate metrics from task success.
  3. Model the human's body, not just their position. The casualty-evacuation planner cuts human energy use by 17.8% versus a human-only baseline by planning with a metabolic model, and CoDance trains on partner forces at both hands. For physical HRI work (handovers, assisted walking, co-carrying), add a human-effort or contact-force term to the evaluation even when the controller does not optimise it, so trade-offs become visible.
  4. Human time is being treated as the scarce resource in robot learning. Reward-DAgger gates human interventions with a general-purpose progress model, and Mulligan starts collection episodes at observed failures, raising real-task success by 10-34 points at matched budgets. The infant-caregiver simulator points the other way, using robot simulation to study how social touch shapes development. If your team collects demonstrations or runs user studies, plan the operator's and participant's time as explicitly as compute.

    Abstract

  • Frame-Level Temporal Alignment for Human-to-Robot Visual Adaptation RO
    Xizhe Zhang, Jingfeng Zhang, Zirun Zhou, Hong Jia · arXiv:2610.04372
    Abstract

    Transferring visual representations pretrained on human videos to robot manipulation requires learning reliable correspondences between human and robot demonstrations. However, paired demonstrations can differ in execution rate and in the proportion of non-key frames that do not directly reflect task progress. Frames at the same relative timestamp may therefore represent different task stages, which can cause correspondence learning to fail. To address these issues, we propose Frame-Level Temporal Alignment (FLTA), a framework that uses two temporal priors to adapt visual encoders pretrained on human videos for robot manipulation. It learns shared task-progress representations without frame-level correspondence annotations, allowing frames at different relative temporal positions to match. A global progress prior combines normalized temporal positions with visual similarity to construct a soft correspondence target. A local temporal order prior penalizes backward transitions while allowing stays and varying forward rates to accommodate execution-rate differences. With ResNet-50 and ViT encoders, our method achieves relative improvements of 46.93% and 65.96%, respectively, over the best baselines in average simulation success rates and achieves higher task success rates on real-world manipulation tasks. These results also suggest that effective human-robot adaptation depends less on the number of parameters updated than on which parameters are selected.   • An End-to-End Framework for Modelling Pneumatic Soft Robots Based on Differentiable Finite Element Methods RO
    Shaohong Zhong, Yao Yao, Perla Maiolino, Ingmar Posner · arXiv:2610.04612
    Abstract

    Soft robots present significant modelling challenges due to their non-linearity, complex dynamics and potentially intricate geometries. Prior modelling approaches typically suffer from trade-offs in accuracy, computational efficiency, or speed.   • Test-Time Training as Residual Memory for Robot Policies RO
    Haoxuan Wang, Gengyu Zhang, Ramana Rao Kompella, Gaowen Liu et al. · arXiv:2610.04701
    Abstract

    Memory is essential for long-horizon robotic manipulation, where successful actions may depend on past events that are no longer recoverable from the current observation. As episodes grow longer, however, retaining the full history becomes increasingly costly, creating a fundamental scalability challenge for memory-augmented policies. Existing approaches address this challenge by either storing selected past observations in a bounded memory bank or compressing interaction history into a fixed-size parametric state through Test-Time Training (TTT). Yet these formulations do not explicitly distinguish between historical information that can already be recovered from the policy's current context and information that must persist beyond it. We introduce TTT-RM, which repurposes TTT as Residual Memory, using fast weights not to generically compress history but to complement a bounded memory bank by preserving task-relevant historical information that cannot be recovered from the policy's current context. Concretely, TTT-RM learns a history decoder that reconstructs historical representations from the current observation and retrieved memory. The resulting reconstruction residual captures what this context fails to explain and serves as the learning target for TTT. The TTT slow weights are optimized against this residual target so that online fast-weight updates learn to encode complementary historical information over time. The fast-weight state is then queried to produce a residual memory representation that conditions action generation. Extensive experiments on memory-intensive simulation benchmarks and real-world tasks show that TTT-RM consistently improves across multiple memory designs, outperforms diverse baselines, and supports sustained execution on a three-minute, eight-stage stowing task.

  • Robot Learning with Visual Predicted Force RO/AI
    Haonan Chen, Feiyang Wu, Yuxiang Ma, Mustafa Mete et al. · arXiv:2610.04741
    Abstract

    Force-aware manipulation typically relies on specialized force or tactile sensors.     Abstract
