The human side of robot learning is the bottleneck today: simulated people misjudge methods, home agents over-act, and stated traits predict little.

Today's papers point at the same weak link: the human in the loop is modelled, labelled or reported in ways that do not match real people. Q-OIL and Goodhart in the Planner show that simulated interventions and synthetic preference labels change which methods look good and how far a planner can be pushed before the task fails. HomeLoop and Relic show that home and companion agents fail by acting too much and by remembering without governance, and the self-report and tone studies suggest that what a model says about itself or how it sounds is a weak guide to what it will do. On the capability side, agentic harnesses around general models now beat specialist VLAs on public benchmarks, which makes the evaluation question more urgent, not less.

Insights

  1. Fix the human-feedback model before tuning the policy. Q-OIL's intervention logs show that people step in when progress stalls and then correct suboptimally, which a generic simulated expert does not reproduce. Goodhart in the Planner shows the cost of ignoring this: the harder the planner searches against a learned preference scorer, the more true return it loses, with human-label drawer-open falling to 0 of 20 runs. Try the progress-based intervention model in your own sim benchmark, and check any preference-trained planner against task success on held-out real trials.
  2. For home and companion agents, the test to add is over-action. HomeLoop's results show that a success-only score hides the failure that matters at home: models act on prohibited objects such as the stove before they know the cause of an alarm. Relic offers a design pattern for the memory side, keeping what the agent may say separate from what researchers can review, and its pilot of two people is explicitly too small to support claims about usability. Score prohibited interventions as hard failures and keep memory writes tied to user confirmation.
  3. Treat agentic harnesses as a candidate baseline, and judge them on failure rate. AMP1's real-world result of 104 of 108 successful trials is strong, but the abstract does not report cost, latency or safety checks for a general model that commands the arm directly. Run the harness against your current VLA on the same task suite, with the same hardware, and log latency per decision and every unsafe attempt, then decide which parts of the stack should stay specialist.

Goodhart in the Planner: Sampling-Based Control Exploits Rewards Learned from Human Preferences RO/AI
Soovadeep Bakshi, Apurba Bose · arXiv:2611.09670
Why read it
A team that trains a planner on human preference labels can see its reward model score well while task success falls; this paper quantifies that loss and gives a fix to test.
Key idea
Harder sampling-based search (MPPI) moves the planner away from its proposal distribution and exploits errors in the preference scorer. A chi-squared-regularised MPPI, with temperature set from the scorer's error spread, is the safest fix tested.
Evidence
True return drops by up to 76% under harder search; drawer-open with human labels succeeds in 0 of 20 runs against 10 of 10 for the cloned policy; held-out label accuracy stays at 0.75-0.97; the fix keeps 86-94% of the cloned policy's return on plate-slide and drawer-open.
Caveat
Tests are on HalfCheetah, Hopper and two Meta-World tasks. The fix is calibrated from 100 proposal pairs ranked by true return (an oracle), which a robot team may not have. Abstract-only reading.

  • CS-Pi: Constraint-Satisfying Flow Policy Sampling RO
    Soumojit Bhattacharya, Oliver Kroemer, Shubham Tulsiani · arXiv:2611.08654
    Abstract

    A flow-matching policy deployed in the field must often satisfy a constraint that was absent from its demonstrations, such as avoiding an obstacle it has never seen. Retraining for every new constraint is impractical, so the constraint must be enforced at inference time. Enforcing it must not discard what the policy learned: an action the policy would rarely sample may avoid the obstacle yet fail the task. We therefore want actions that are both safe, satisfying the constraint, and plausible, remaining likely under the policy. Gradient guidance, the most common approach, steers noisy states, so it must evaluate its cost on the policy's running estimate of the clean action; when the policy is uncertain between passing left or right of an obstacle, this estimate is their average and can pass straight through it. We instead build on rejection and projection. Rejection redraws actions until one is safe: every output is plausible, but the wait is intractable when safe actions are rare. Projection moves an action to the nearest safe one: it is efficient, but the result need not be plausible. Our key insight is that flow-matching sampling lets us combine the two, keeping projection's efficiency and rejection's plausibility. CS-Pi, our draw-project-renoise sampler, projects each draw to the nearest safe action, adds noise back scaled to how far the projection moved it, and re-runs the unmodified policy until a draw is safe. Replacing only the projection step extends CS-Pi to continuous costs. Across a 2D diagnostic, a 7-DoF shelf pick-and-place, an orientation preference, and a zero-shot Franka deployment, CS-Pi reaches 81% safe success on the shelf and 55% on hardware, against 55% and 40% for projection, while staying plausible.

  • Lodos: A Lightweight Simulator Designed for Contact-Rich Interactions with Detailed Meshes RO
    Onur Beker, Andreas René Geist, Georg Martius · arXiv:2611.08872
    Abstract

    In recent years, massively parallelized rigid-body simulation has played a central role in robotics research, enabling rapid progress in areas such as reinforcement learning, stochastic optimal control, and randomized smoothing-based trajectory optimization. Particularly in contact-rich settings, the scalability of such simulations in large part depends on well-optimized representations of collision geometry, often requiring a coarse approximation of the underlying surfaces using primitives such as spheres, capsules, cylinders, and boxes. Whenever the complexity of the surfaces or the precision demands of the application render such approximations insufficient, it is instead necessary to employ mesh-mesh collisions, which impose a significant reduction in throughput. This paper proposes a simulation framework designed from scratch to address this bottleneck, with novel routines for broadphase and narrowphase collision detection as well as the subsequent contact dynamics computation. As experimentally validated, the framework matches existing well-established simulators in terms of physical faithfulness, and can achieve approximately an order of magnitude higher throughput in the detailed mesh-mesh collision setting that it specializes in.   • HOC2R: Hand-Object Contact Trajectories for Human-to-Robot Motion Transfer RO
    Hongkun Yu, Francisco M. López, Francisco Cruz · arXiv:2611.08920
    Abstract

    Human videos contain abundant object-manipulation demonstrations, but they do not directly provide motion cues that a robot can follow. We present HOC2R, a Hand-Object Contact trajectory extractor for Robot motion that combines frozen DINOv3 and SAM 2.1 features with learned heads for hand-object association and contact localization. HOC2R supports two modes: count-aware automation that detects, counts, and localizes visible contacts in untrimmed videos, and fixed-two localization for two-finger grippers when contact frames are known. On a 30-task point-annotated benchmark, count-aware automation improves contact-count accuracy from 58.1% to 71.4% and reduces mean localization distance from 19.81 to 10.46 pixels relative to an adapted VRB2 baseline. On seven unseen RGB-D rollouts, a five-task fixed-two checkpoint reduces mean localization distance from 67.67 to 27.02 pixels. Across six robot evaluation sequences, the 30-task fixed-two checkpoint produces four feasible trajectories and succeeds on three tasks, compared with two feasible trajectories and one successful task for each adapted baseline. HOC2R is competitive in the evaluated single-hand scenes, robust to the hand-color shift, and strongest in the evaluated multi-hand scenes. These results support HOC2R as a practical pseudo-labeling method for extracting robot-usable contact trajectories from human demonstrations.

  • Robust Readout of Cross-Point Resistive Arrays via Time-Domain Optimization RO

    Abstract

  • Horizon-Coupled Obstacle Guidance for Relative-Action Diffusion Policies RO
    Guangyuan Wang, Haishan Xia, Zitao Zhang, Kejia Chen et al. · arXiv:2611.09340
    Abstract

    Real-world robot deployment requires adapting learned policies to unseen obstacles without collecting new demonstrations or retraining. Existing policy-level guidance often applies obstacle corrections independently at individual horizon steps, which may address local obstacle violations but fail to coordinate the action sequence into a complete, task-directed detour. How obstacle responses should be propagated across the action horizon to coordinate complete, task-directed avoidance motion remains underexplored. We introduce an inferencetime obstacle-guidance framework for pretrained relative-action diffusion policies. It rolls out cumulative relative actions into a workspace trajectory, evaluates obstacle interactions along the trajectory with an SDF-based obstacle model, and propagates each obstacle response to all contributing action increments during denoising. Ablations show that the relative-action horizoncoupled formulation provides the larger observed improvement across both obstacle models.

  • Seeing What Matters: Benchmarking Task-Conditioned Actionable Spatial Belief for Manipulation RO/CV
    Yina Jian, Mingkun Xu · arXiv:2611.09794
    Abstract

    When should a robot look at the cup, and when at the portafilter? Spatial-reasoning benchmarks evaluate whether a model can describe a scene, but manipulation needs something more specific: the ability to acquire the particular evidence that makes the current command executable. A view that is informative for one task can be useless for another, even at the same workstation. We introduce Seeing What Matters, a benchmark for task-conditioned actionable spatial belief in simulated tabletop manipulation. Across three domains--coffee workstation, drawer/cabinet, and microwave/appliance--the benchmark provides 860 frozen evaluation cases; expanding across methods, budgets, and inference repeats yields more than 115,000 evaluator episodes under two protocols: closed-loop actionability and same-object functional out-of-distribution generalization. Replacing the reference pipeline's coverage metadata with frozen RGB-derived scores preserves 98.5%/98.9% success; shuffling the visual scores collapses it to 15%/11%, confirming that the result depends on image content. These gaps suggest that spatial belief for manipulation should be evaluated not by scene-level completeness, but by whether it acquires the evidence the current command requires.

  • Grasp-TAGS: Task-Aware Grasp Selection via Critic Distillation for Prehensile Loco-Manipulation RO/AI

    Abstract

Despite recent progress, reliably executing these tasks remains challenging because performance depends on complex robot-object and object-environment interaction dynamics. Existing approaches address this challenge either through model-based methods, which often require accurate dynamics models, or through learning-based methods, which reduce this requirement but typically assume a fixed or predefined initial grasp.
