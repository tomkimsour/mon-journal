Social and safety behaviour is being learned into robot policies, with training-only signals doing much of the work while evaluation lags behind.

Today's papers share one shift: the signals that make robots social, safe and cheap to run are increasingly used during training and dropped at deployment. Humanoid papers make partner motion and intent explicit inputs, while VLA papers move foresight and alignment into losses or branches that are removed at inference. The same pattern shows up in runtime monitoring, where failure-mode prediction with bounds replaces a single failure score. The weak link is evaluation: criteria that shift slightly, and self-reported success, can make a result look stronger than the robot's real behaviour. For a team shipping humanoids, the practical work is to instrument intent, latency and held-out verifiers together.

Insights

  1. The most useful near-term change for a humanoid team is to make social signals explicit inputs and hard gates. Social Humanoid conditions on partner motion through a shared token space and anticipates occluded partners, and AffectiveVLA raises intent macro-F1 from 0.75 to 0.90 while holding motion when confidence is low. Try an intent gate on your own hardware and measure how often it suppresses correct motion before adding more interaction data. The evidence here is from a Unitree G1 with a 1,300-episode VR teleoperation dataset, and the abstracts do not report how many on-robot trials were run.
  2. Training-only components are where the cost of foresight now sits. La-JEPA, SLA, NegWAM and the navigation work all drop their predictors, teachers or branches at inference. La-JEPA reports state-of-the-art results among policies without embodied pretraining on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO-Plus, and comparable average success to pi0.5 on four real-robot tasks. Most of that evidence is simulation, and the authors corrected the LIBERO-Plus instructions themselves, so treat the numbers as a hypothesis for your own robot and benchmark them on your own tasks.
  3. Evaluation design is the weak point. Criteria Exposure shows gains that vanish when the verifier shifts slightly, and RoboARCade's agents overclaim success on 24% to 39% of subtasks. Stop reporting a single in-distribution success rate. Hold out one verifier variant and one physical parameter, then report the drop. The abstracts' evidence comes from controlled simulation experiments, so the size of this effect on a real robot is unknown.
  4. Human judgement of robot motion is worth collecting as a signal. The navigation work reports that naive observers rate its motion as more human-like, and the cyclist study converts crowd safety ratings into per-feature values, such as +0.97 rating points for physical lane separation. Its inter-annotator agreement is alpha 0.4695, which means the labels are noisy. Collect ratings from several annotators and report agreement alongside every result.

Why read it
It is the clearest HRI paper today: it addresses physical coordination with a human partner rather than solitary humanoid motion, on a 29-DoF Unitree G1.
Key idea
A high-level policy generates reactive humanoid motion from a shared tokenized representation of both partners, anticipating the partner when occluded, and a low-level tracker executes it on the robot.

The abstract reports a 260-hour human-humanoid interaction benchmark built from five motion-capture datasets, and states coordinated, human-aligned behaviour in simulation and the real world. It gives no success rate or human rating numbers.
Caveat
The two-phase retargeting morphs one partner to preserve contact, so the benchmark's human motion is altered; the abstract does not say how much this changes contact realism.

Why read it
A runtime monitor that predicts the failure mode and comes with calibrated bounds is directly usable for an on-robot safety layer, and it releases a labelled dataset.
Key idea
A lightweight probe over the VLA backbone's latent layers, trained on the pre-failure segment of trajectories, predicts both the failure and its mode, so the system can choose a corrective action.

Early detection improves by up to 30% at matched false-positive rates over the strongest learned baseline. Under unseen-task shift, the bounds remain valid in up to 86% of runs versus at most 21% for baselines, and top-3 failure-mode accuracy on unseen tasks is up to 44% above a majority baseline.

    Abstract

    Replay attacks are difficult to detect in vision-based control systems because previously recorded observations can remain plausible while the physical system follows a different trajectory. This paper develops an adaptive dynamic-watermarking framework for detecting replay attacks against vision-based controllers. A private Gaussian watermark is added to the nominal control input, and a control input-conditioned latent dynamics network (LDN) predicts the corresponding evolution in a compact latent space learned from image observations.
