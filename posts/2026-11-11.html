Robot results are increasingly being checked against shortcuts, and the human-facing papers today show that social cues are still where current models fall short.

Several papers today test whether a robot's apparent competence comes from the capability it is supposed to show. A kNN retrieval baseline reaches 91.7% on LIBERO, a memory VLA stops early when a wrist fidget is inserted, and the same policies barely change when room light drops 11.5x, because the cameras auto-expose. In the human-facing work, a humanoid negotiator that updates its affect model online gets higher concession rates from 66 participants, and a social robot with emotion-conditioned gestures is preferred by 60 participants, while a child-centric benchmark shows VLMs collapsing on behavioral and interpersonal cues. On the safety side, the common move is to take the decision out of the model: a lease-style gate on each action, a code-driven workflow instead of an LLM choosing each step, and a barrier steering the latent before a path-space filter. For a team building robots that interact with people of different ages and cultures, the practical message is to evaluate with humans and with shortcut-breaking controls, not with the headline benchmark number alone.

Insights

  1. Before trusting a new manipulation number, check whether a shortcut reproduces it. The retrieval result on LIBERO (91.7% with no training, and the smallest drop under LIBERO-Plus perturbations among the evaluated policies) suggests that many current VLA scores measure how well the backbone supports nearest-neighbour lookup in demonstration space. The authors also propose Local Action Consistency as an offline metric that tracks retrieval success and can guide training of the retrieval key. Try it on your own demonstration set before changing the policy architecture, and stop reporting success rate without a retrieval baseline.
  2. Memory and perception modules are where deployment bugs will hide. The memory-VLA paper shows success falling from 47 to 16 of 50 on one counting task when a null pause is inserted, and the lux paper shows that four imitation policies scored 20/40 in normal light and 19/40 in low light, with image brightness only 3% lower on the front camera. Both point at the sensing and memory interface rather than the policy weights. For a human-facing robot, that means logging camera exposure and white balance on every trial, and indexing any memory by events or task progress rather than by elapsed time.
  3. For social robots, the strongest human-facing signal this week is personalization under live feedback. The affect model that updates during negotiation achieved significantly higher individual utility and elicited higher concession rates than a fixed baseline across 66 participants, but participant utility and Nash distance did not change significantly, so the benefit is not a clean win for both sides. Try the differential-feedback idea on a real interaction loop with a mixed-culture participant pool, and check whether the arousal estimates shift across groups before you assume the same calibration transfers.

Is Your Robot's Memory Just a Clock? Temporal Shortcuts in Memory-Augmented VLA Policies RO
Chubin Zhang, Bo An · arXiv:2611.14488
Why read it
It isolates a concrete failure mode that will appear when a memory-augmented policy meets real pauses, retries or operator interventions. The fix it suggests is cheap to test.
Key idea
Time-sampled memory treats elapsed time as progress, so a null event looks like work; indexing memory by events removes most of that error without retraining.
Evidence
On RoboMME counting tasks, success falls from 47 to 16 of 50 on one task and from 44 to 10 on the other when a null pause is inserted. Event-indexed memory recovers half or more of the loss without retraining.
Caveat
All results are on RoboMME simulation tasks, and the abstract reads as a diagnosis more than a deployment test. The claim that training with injected pauses impairs counting comes from the same benchmark.
LEASH: Stay Safe in Agentic Robotics RO
Qinfeng Zhu, Weiguang Zhao, Ke Sun, Xihao Guo et al. · arXiv:2611.14115
Why read it
Gives the team a reference for how much an agentic LLM controller can harm the physical world, and a gate design that can be bolted onto an existing planner.
Key idea
Each task, each action and each irreversible commit goes through a gate that reviews it in its visual context, with a shared safety policy across the planner and gates, using a decision model.
Evidence
RoboRisk covers 92 simulated tasks and 13 physical-robot tasks in five categories and runs every task in closed loop. The abstract reports that the six foundation models generally show high harm rates, and that the harness reduces harm across all six while largely preserving task completion and adding little time and token overhead.

The abstract does not give harm-rate numbers, and the physical-robot task count is small. The model names in the evaluation are as reported by the paper, and the gate depends on a decision model whose own failure modes are not analyzed in the abstract.

  • Robo-MLT: Dual-System Robotic Manipulation for Long-Horizon Medical Laboratory Tasks RO
    Zequan Zeng, Kai Wang, Delvin Ce Zhang, Huaping Liu et al. · arXiv:2611.13039
    Abstract

    Reliable medical laboratory automation requires robots to complete entire workflows in protocol order while handling changes during execution. A robot may execute each motion correctly yet fail the workflow by confusing visually similar stages or stalling after human intervention. We present Robo-MLT, a dual-system framework that couples explicit workflow monitoring with learned action generation. A vision--language planner composes an ordered atomic-skill sequence from the protocol and the initial workspace observation. A multiframe execution monitor detects when the active skill has completed or become infeasible, then advances the workflow or triggers replanning. After an operator removes the sample being handled, the system revises the remaining plan and continues processing the other samples. These results indicate that, under the evaluated conditions, explicit workflow monitoring makes long-horizon laboratory manipulation more reliable and recoverable.

  • Fastener-centric acquisition and AND/OR graph representation of state-dependent disassembly knowledge from CAD-derived virtual assemblies RO
    Shiki Iwase · arXiv:2611.13379
    Abstract

    Design-stage disassemblability assessment requires knowledge of product structure and the operations available in each assembly state, but CAD assemblies do not directly specify state-dependent disassembly operations. This paper proposes a fastener-centric virtual-disassembly framework that acquires such knowledge from CAD-derived virtual assemblies. Fasteners jointly releasing the same constraint are grouped into operation-level sets, avoiding enumeration of arbitrary individual-fastener orders.   • CIALLO: Learning Shared Intent Representations for Cross-Embodiment Manipulation RO
    Choo Sin Wai, Dake Zhong, Jingye Zhang, Zhifang Liu et al. · arXiv:2611.13732
    Abstract

    Robot demonstrations contain reusable manipulation knowledge, but transferring this knowledge across embodiments requires representations that bridge differences in control interfaces. Action reconstruction alone does not ensure that similar behaviors across robots share a representation. To address this gap, we introduce CIALLO, an action codec that aligns actions, visual changes, and observation-grounded language in a shared space of short-horizon behavioral intent. An embodiment-conditioned decoder maps this intent back to robot-specific actions, allowing a shared representation to support different control interfaces. The LIBERO policy also achieves 80.8\% on LIBERO-Plus without further finetuning. Together, these results support a shared action codec as a transferable control interface that separates embodiment calibration from task learning.

  • LaST0.5: A Generalist World Action Model with Interaction-Centric Latent Reasoning RO
    Xuheng Zhang, Yiming Zhang, Jialin Gao, Hao Chen et al. · arXiv:2611.14048
    Abstract

    World Action Models (WAMs) have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for robotic manipulation, endowing policies with strong capabilities for modeling temporal and physical dynamics. Existing WAMs, encompassing both video-first action prediction and joint video-action modeling, directly transfer global physical representations from world models to action generation.   • Model-Free Runtime Measurement of Task-Relevant Information RO/AI
    Dimitris Panagopoulos, Adolfo Perrusquía, Yang Xing, Weisi Guo · arXiv:2611.14111
    Abstract

    Autonomous systems increasingly monitor physical kinematics, environmental states and task completion outcomes. While existing work provides methods to plan information seeking actions and interpret acquired observations, current approaches do not monitor whether the live process of information gathering is functioning properly. Consequently, such systems can remain operationally active without resolving outstanding mission requirements. To address this limitation, we introduce Acquisition-State Telemetry (AST), a model free architecture that evaluates ongoing information acquisition from observable evidence relative to declared task requirements. Specifically, a domain interface maps raw observations into task resolution coordinates and normalized comparative representations, which are processed by a fixed causal monitor.     Abstract
