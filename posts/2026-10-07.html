Today's most useful results come from better controls and readouts, not bigger models: a scripted retreat matches a learned VLA corrector, reading logits beats a fine-tuned emotion model, and robots that read human actions help about as well as robots that are told what to do.

Several papers today show that how something is measured or read out changes the conclusion more than the model does: a paired placebo shrinks the value of a learned VLA corrector, a logit readout turns 'VLMs fail at fine-grained emotion' into 'VLMs beat the fine-tuned model', and a compact policy matches systems 40-160x larger once its visual encoder is controlled. On the HRI side, assistance is increasingly triggered by what the person does (an action's outcome, a hand pose, a push on a carried load) rather than by an explicit instruction, while LLM-driven robots become easier to supervise when their plans are exposed as typed, structured commitments. In manipulation, contact feedback is being added to vision-first policies through the cheapest available channel, and a growing runtime layer decides how much of a frozen policy's action chunk to trust. World models keep showing the same gap: video that looks right while actions, hidden objects or unstable modes are wrong. For a team building interactive robots, the common thread is to test the specific property you rely on (the human cue, the contact event, the occluded object) with a control condition, before adding learned machinery.

Insights

  1. Run the boring control before adding learned recovery. On frozen π0.5, 43.2% of failed episodes succeeded on a plain same-seed re-run, and a fixed-time trigger with a scripted return to an earlier joint configuration produced a net gain with no detected difference from the learned corrector (2610.06921). The quantized-agent study shows the same fragility on the language side: one scoring policy turned a +28.3 point advantage into -15.0 (2610.07781). Add a same-seed re-run and a scripted retreat to your evaluation scripts, and report results per task rather than averaged.
  2. For social-perception modules, change how you read the model before you fine-tune one. Asking a VLM one yes/no question per emotion category and using the probability, not the generated text, put all eleven tested open-weight models above expert agreement on the five most reliable categories (2610.08162), and a psychology-derived probe shows VLMs shift from surface object matching to relational matching with capability, model scale, and fewer, noise-free objects per scene (2610.07646). The replication on real photographs was weaker and mixed, so validate any affect estimator on real faces from the age groups and cultures you deploy to before trusting it.
  3. Robots with a wheeled base and lift need their own transfer pipeline from human data. Retargeting that assumes legs fails for mobile-base humanoids, and a responsibility allocator that splits walking into base motion and torso bending into lift-plus-arm motion is one practical alternative (2610.08381). MobileVISTA adds that centimetre-scale base pose errors break manipulation policies, most on platforms whose camera rides the actuated chain (2610.07511). If you collect teleop or human-video demonstrations, augment over base pose and log the base/lift/arm split explicitly.
  4. Wire contact signals you already have into the policy now. Gripper current and position improved choosing the higher-resistance object from 48.8% to 75.0% with no extra sensor (2610.08425), and simply showing tactile numbers to an untrained VLM raised peg insertion from 10/20 to 15/20 (2610.07621). For robots that hand objects to people, this is a cheap route to gentler, more predictable grasps.

The Failure Is in the Readout: Fine-Grained Emotion Recognition Benchmarks Measure Elicitation, Not Perception CV
Tobias Hallmen, Fabian Deuser, Robin-Nico Kampa, Norbert Oswald et al. · arXiv:2610.08162
Why read it
Emotion recognition feeds therapy tools and social robots; this paper shows a benchmark's 'VLMs are not good enough, fine-tune a dedicated model' conclusion came from the readout protocol, which changes what an HRI team should build.
Key idea
Keep the images, taxonomy and expert ratings, but ask an off-the-shelf VLM one binary question per emotion category and read the graded yes-probability from the logits instead of letting it generate an answer.
Evidence
On EmoNet-Face-HQ (40 categories, generated portraits), experts agree at κw = 0.468 on the five most reliable categories. Generatively, no interval among eleven open-weight VLMs lies entirely above that anchor; under verification all eleven clear it (κw = 0.507-0.586), and three significantly beat the fine-tuned EIF model (κw = 0.551 Small, 0.534 Large). Thresholding the same probabilities to yes/no drops performance to κw = 0.254-0.423.
Caveat
Main results are on synthetic portraits; the replication on real photographs (FACES) is weaker and mixed (six of ten models gain, one gets worse). The abstract gives no breakdown by age or culture of the faces, which matters for a diverse user population.
Reactive Task-Oriented Robot-Human Handovers via Generative Hypothesis Selection RO
Carmen Scheidemann, Andreea Tulbure, Pascal Burkhardt, Marco Hutter · arXiv:2610.08003
Why read it
Handovers are among the most frequent physical interactions a service robot has with people, and many objects can be used in more than one way, so the right handover pose depends on what the person intends.
Key idea
Use VLM image generation to produce several task-specific hand-object interaction hypotheses, then match them in real time to the observed human hand pose to infer which handover configuration the person expects.
Evidence
The interaction proposal module is evaluated on its own, then the full system is deployed on a mobile manipulator. In a user study with 12 participants across five task-object pairs, 83.3% perceived it as having better task understanding than the previous state of the art.
Caveat
Small study (12 participants, five task-object pairs) measuring perceived task understanding, not handover success rate, timing or comfort; the abstract reports no latency or failure cases for the generative step.
MRPilot: Supervising and Intervening LLM-Based Multi-Robot Teams through Mixed Reality RO
Xiaoran Yang, Xun Qian, Yang Zhan, Nathan Tran et al. · arXiv:2610.07477
Why read it
As LLMs start dispatching robot teams, people need to see and fix what the planner decided; this is one of the few papers today with a controlled user study on trust and workload.
Key idea
Represent LLM-generated multi-robot plans and execution states as structured commitments shown in synchronised situated and overview mixed-reality views, organised into Forming, Reviewing, Following and Repairing stages of supervision.
Evidence
Designed from a formative study with 12 non-expert users; in a within-subjects study with 20 participants in a VR-simulated home, it reduced workload and increased situational awareness, transparency, trust and perceived control compared with a conversational interface using the same LLM planner and robot capabilities.
Caveat
Robots and home were simulated in VR; the abstract reports no task success or effect sizes, and does not show whether higher trust was calibrated to actual plan correctness.
Does a Learned Corrector Beat a Simple Retreat? Evidence from a Frozen VLA RO
Chenchao Sheng, Zhuang Jiang, Liuhaichen Yang, Ningwei Bai et al. · arXiv:2610.06921
Why read it
Anyone planning to add a learned recovery module to a VLA should read this first: it sets out what evidence is needed to show the module helps beyond run-to-run variation.
Key idea
Evaluate runtime interventions with paired same-seed rollouts, a same-seed base-policy re-run as a placebo, and a simple scripted retreat as a baseline.
Evidence
Across 3,888 paired episodes on frozen π0.5 and four RoboTwin tasks, the full pipeline raises success by +13.5 pp on one task (95% interval [+9.4, +17.7]) with no detectable gain on the other three. 43.2% of failed base episodes succeed on a plain re-run versus 63.5% after correction. A fixed-time trigger plus scripted return produces a net gain with no detected difference from the learned pipeline.
Caveat
Simulation only (RoboTwin) and a single policy (π0.5); the prespecified equivalence criterion between scripted retreat and learned correction was not met consistently, so 'equivalent' is not established.
From Legs to Wheels: Embodiment-Aware Human Motion Retargeting for Mobile-Base Humanoids RO
Jiyeon Koo, Eunseom Pyo, Jeonghee Seo, Taehwa Kim et al. · arXiv:2610.08381
Why read it
Directly relevant to mobile-base humanoids with a wheeled base, vertical lift and two arms, which most human-to-humanoid retargeting work ignores because it assumes legs.
Key idea
Allocate reconstructed human motion to base, lift and arm responsibilities before robot-specific realisation (walking becomes base motion, torso bending becomes coordinated lift and arm motion), then convert to 50 Hz commands with stationary-base detection, deadband and slew-rate filtering and playback scaling.

  • AIM: Adaptive Interaction Modeling Networks for Real-to-Sim Soft-Body Simulation RO
    Tiancheng Yang, Dingshuo Chen, Tianle Chen, Zhaocheng Liu et al. · arXiv:2610.07116
    Abstract

    Deformable-object manipulation is essential for robotic tasks such as folding laundry and handling food, where robots must control shape changes as well as object motion. Predictive soft-body simulation supports these tasks by anticipating deformation under external interactions. However, spatial neighborhoods can misrepresent deformation dependencies, introducing local errors that accumulate over successive predictions. Models fitted to individual scenes must also accommodate changes in object geometry and manipulation conditions. In this work, we propose AIM, an Adaptive Interaction Modeling framework that treats real-to-sim soft-body simulation as a local-global interaction modeling problem. AIM uses motion history and geometry to adapt particle relations over current spatial neighbors and retained connections, while geometry-conditioned global communication coordinates object-wide responses. A unified kinematic control-point interface represents different manipulation configurations, and multi-step autoregressive supervision trains the model on its own predicted trajectories. Experiments on PhysTwin and PGND demonstrate improved motion accuracy and visual fidelity, with a 20.0% reduction in future-prediction tracking error relative to PhysTwin and a 22.8% reduction in mean long-horizon particle error across six object categories relative to PGND. The framework further supports transfer across actions, object instances, and scenes, including zero-shot transfer from robot interactions to human manipulation without target-domain dynamics fitting.

  • Task-Space Imitation Guidance for Efficient Reinforcement Learning RO
    Salar Asayesh, Hossein Darani, Todd Cao, Evgeny Andriash et al. · arXiv:2610.07527
    Abstract

    We introduce Task-Space Imitation Guidance for Efficient Reinforcement Learning (TIGER), a reward-construction and pretraining framework for sparse-reward tabletop robotic manipulation. TIGER treats an action-chunked imitation policy not as an executable controller or action prior, but as a local task-space progress estimator: predicted action chunks are converted, using controller-aware action-to-motion mapping, into short-horizon end-effector references, and the RL agent receives dense progress rewards toward these references while the sparse environment reward remains the dominant objective. During pretraining, TIGER uses imitation-guided look-ahead signals to relax conservative value penalties for actions predicted to make task-space progress, reducing off-manifold exploration during early online RL. Across simulation and real-robot experiments, TIGER improves early sample efficiency and reduces measured safety violations while matching or improving final success rates relative to prior RL and IL-RL baselines on the evaluated tasks.

  • Learning Grasp Targeting from Point Clouds for Log Pile Clearing on a Hydraulic Crane RO
    George Sideris, Lucas Bessai, Heshan Fernando, Elie Ayoub et al. · arXiv:2610.07613
    Abstract

    In mill yards, log loaders clear dense piles by a sequence of bundle grasps: hundreds of logs rest in contact, and each removal changes the pile available to the next grasp. A learned policy chooses where to place and orient the grapple from unsegmented point clouds and runs on a trailer-mounted hydraulic forestry crane. The policy classifies at which observed point to grasp and predicts depth and grapple orientation there. The same network outputs support behavior cloning (BC), reinforcement learning (RL), and deployment. BC learns from successful top-of-pile demonstrations; RL explores for improvements by fine-tuning the cloned policy (BC$\to$RL) or by training from scratch.   • EigenDEXplore: Structured Exploration for Dexterous Manipulation with Human Priors RO/AI
    Harsh Gupta, Tyler Ga Wei Lum, Changhao Wang, Chuer Pan et al. · arXiv:2610.07681
    Abstract

    Dexterous manipulation poses a challenging high-dimensional optimization problem, as useful behaviors require coordinated motion across many hand joints. In reinforcement learning (RL) and sampling-based trajectory optimization, exploration commonly relies on independent robot joint perturbations, making coordinated behaviors difficult to discover. Prior work reduces this search space for grasp learning using low-dimensional spaces of coordinated joint motions learned from human hand data, but this restricts the expressivity required for general manipulation. Some combine learned and joint-space actions to restore expressivity, but this increases dimensionality and introduces redundancy. We study these effects across diverse manipulation settings, varying action dimensionality, exploration strategy, and the source of human data. Our experiments suggest that human-motion priors are most effective when used to structure exploration rather than change the action representation. Motivated by this finding, we propose EigenDEXplore, which induces correlated exploration by adding perturbations along human-derived eigenvectors to independent joint-space noise, leaving the action space unchanged. Across multiple dexterous hands, EigenDEXplore consistently outperforms joint-space and learned action-space baselines in grasping, in-hand reorientation, and contact-rich manipulation. These gains span unstructured and reference-guided RL, trajectory optimization, and sim-to-real deployment, and are largest in settings with less reward shaping and curriculum design.

  • ExoBridge: Learning a Bare Hand to Hand-Worn Exoskeleton Mapping through Human Limb Coupling RO
    Ruitong Tian, Xianyao Li, Noah B. Wilson, Fang Xu et al. · arXiv:2610.07692
    Abstract

    Human video offers a scalable source of experience for dexterous robot learning, but obtaining motion and tactile supervision while preserving bare hand interaction remains challenging. We present ExoBridge, a framework that leverages human limb coupling to learn a bridging function from bare hand video to the motion and tactile state of a sensorized exoskeleton. Our central idea is to use coordinated bimanual behavior to connect an uninstrumented visual source with a measured manipulation interface. During collection, one hand remains bare and provides visual observations, while the opposite hand wears the exoskeleton and supplies synchronized motion and tactile measurements. These paired demonstrations train a temporal visual model to predict fingertip contact, continuous tactile intensity, and relative encoder motion from bare hand video alone. The exoskeleton defines an intermediate state space whose motion coordinates are linked to a dexterous robot hand through existing calibration. Evaluation on 1,215 demonstrations across four manipulation tasks uses held out collection sessions and yields a pooled any contact AUROC of 0.916 and a Pearson correlation of 0.790 for tactile intensity. The learned bridge also predicts relative changes in exoskeleton configuration from bare hand video. These results demonstrate that human limb coupling can turn exoskeleton measurements into supervision for bare hand video, establishing a learned bridge between human visual demonstrations and a robot oriented manipulation interface.

  • CoRE: Learning Collaboration-Role Experts for Decentralized Collaborative Manipulation with One Policy RO
    Yanan Zhou, Zhaoyan Qian, Zihao Li, Mingyuan Ba et al. · arXiv:2610.07752
    Abstract

    Collaborative manipulation requires robots to perform complementary actions as interactions unfold. We study single-policy decentralized collaboration: every robot runs the same policy from its visual observations and proprioception, without task prompts, identity labels, or inter-robot messages. The challenge is to learn complementary team behaviors within shared parameters and select appropriate actions from each robot's local observations.
