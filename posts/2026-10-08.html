Robots and the models around them keep believing they finished, understood or judged correctly when they did not, and today's working fixes are explicit check layers outside the model: rephrasing rules, verified counterfactual failures, deterministic scorers and sparse human edits.

The same gap shows up across manipulation, agents, world models and HRI today. VLAs act as if a grasp happened, multimodal agents declare unfinished tasks done, world models predict success on verified failures, a one-word change of phrasing swings success by tens of points, and VLM verdicts about people and hazards track the prompt, the caption bank or the serving stack. The papers that report clear gains do not mainly scale the model. They add a layer that checks or reshapes what goes in and out: instruction rewriting rules, verified counterfactual failure data, an event table read by a deterministic scorer, verification gates before a self-revised skill or policy is adopted, and sparse mid-task human input that the robot integrates without replanning. For a team building robots for people of different ages and cultures, this points to auditing the interface between people and models as carefully as the policy itself.

Insights

  1. Judge a world model by what the controller can do with its prediction, not by how the video looks. Kuration finds qualitative playability does not track FVD, LPIPS or JEDi (2610.09305); Entity-Level Goal Readout makes the SE(3) terminal goal an explicit output because predicted futures do not expose it (2610.09309); RobotAPO targets physics violations at the contact boundary and reports a 37.4% relative real-replay gain (2610.09454); and RoboJEPA reports that latent imagination error correlates strongly with planning performance (2610.10515). If you evaluate world models internally, add one control-facing metric (goal error, success-vs-failure discrimination) next to the visual ones.
  2. When adding touch to a policy, pass physical quantities rather than a raw sensor image. The factorized tactile framework separates contact geometry, force distribution and temporal change and reports a 35% real-world gain on adversarial peg insertion (2610.10510); FACE expresses force in contact-relative coordinates with online normal and friction estimates (2610.09533); a DigiTac-only EKF recovers the hybrid force/motion task frame (2610.10020); and the exoskeleton study finds spatial-plus-force input beats a binary contact signal on four tasks (2610.08870). Start with contact location and force channels before training end-to-end on tactile images.
  3. Decision latency is a safety variable for robots near people. In RT-Safe, letting the world keep moving during VLM inference left task completion similar (91.3% static vs 94.1% real-time) but raised collisions 12.3x (2610.09294). On the control side, RFPO runs a flow policy in one Euler step at 0.08 ms instead of 4.39 ms with returns within 2.4% of 64-step execution (2610.10453). Evaluate any VLM-in-the-loop navigation or interaction with the scene running during inference, not paused.
  4. Personal assistance needs a memory of who is who and what belongs to whom, and smaller models guess too eagerly. COOL infers object ownership from long-term observation of human-object interactions (2610.09358), and the identity-grounded video work finds smaller models over-assign known identities when the queried person is not grounded, while combining face boxes with text coordinates helps across scales (2610.10163). Test your assistant on the case where the person is unknown, and check it says so rather than naming someone.

Rephrase Before You Act: Characterizing and Mitigating Language Sensitivity in Vision-Language-Action Models RO
Mikey Watts, Yuchen Cui · arXiv:2610.10526
Why read it
A robot that takes spoken commands from people of different ages and language backgrounds will hear 'hot plate' as often as 'stove'. This paper measures how much that costs and gives a fix that needs no retraining.
Key idea
Sensitivity to phrasing is systematic, so it can be written as rules: score many phrasings of a few training tasks, have an LLM distill the evidence into ten to twenty rephrasing rules, and rewrite each incoming instruction once at deployment.
Evidence
π0.5 turns on a LIBERO stove 100% of the time for 'switch on the stove' and 2% for 'switch on the hot plate'; a π0 checkpoint fine-tuned with rephrase augmentation still swings up to 61 points. An oracle phrase search nearly closes the 21-point in- vs out-of-distribution gap. The rules improve frozen π0 by 16-27% relative on twelve held-out tasks across adversarial, VLM-generated and human-generated phrasings, and lift π0.5 in-finetune success on LIBERO from 93.6% to 97.8%.
Caveat
Results are on LIBERO-style simulated tasks with π0 and π0.5; the abstract does not test spoken (ASR) input, languages other than English or real robots, and rules derived for one checkpoint likely need regenerating for another.
World Models Dream of Success: Diagnosing and Repairing Failure Insensitivity in Robot World Models RO
Jiuyi Xu, Xiao Hu, Meida Chen, Peng Gao et al. · arXiv:2610.09134
Why read it
If you use a world model to score policies or generate synthetic data, it is probably optimistic about failures. This paper quantifies that on released checkpoints and gives a data recipe to repair them without changing the architecture.
Key idea
Perturb actions from successful demonstrations along a severity grid, verify each outcome by actually executing it, and fine-tune the released model on the verified failures and surviving successes alongside nominal data.
Evidence
Across four released checkpoints from two architecture families, optimism on 484 held-out LIBERO failure counterfactuals falls from 80% (fine-tuned on official data) to 30-43% (38% mean). On a physical arm, failures scored as success-like drop from 90% to 33%. With matched budgets, counterfactual failures give a success-failure value gap of 0.124 versus 0.014 for freshly collected on-policy failures.
Caveat
Every counterfactual must be executed to verify its outcome, which costs simulator or robot time; real-robot evidence is one arm, and the authors themselves warn that lower optimism must be read alongside success-failure discrimination.
A Scoping Review and Experimental Study on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback for Human-Robot Collaboration RO/AI
Alexandra Coroiu, Andrea Vogt, Viktor Werbilo, Andreas Poppele et al. · arXiv:2610.09891
Why read it
Directly on HRI and psychology: it shows that who decides when feedback is given changes whether the feedback reflects how safe people actually feel around the robot.
Key idea
Map RLHF for human-robot collaboration as a closed loop, then test one gap: compare system-initiated and user-initiated feedback on robot proxemic behaviour, linking it to psychological safety (questionnaire) and physical safety (inverse time-to-collision).
Evidence
PRISMA scoping review screened 199 records and kept 20 peer-reviewed papers (2020-2025). A between-subjects VR experiment analysed with Bayesian models found user-initiated feedback captures perceived safety better than system-initiated feedback.
Caveat
The experiment is in VR on one navigation proxemics task; the abstract gives no sample size, effect size or participant age and cultural background, so how far it holds for physical robots and diverse users is open.
A Deterministic Evidence Layer for Vision-Language Autism Screening from Naturalistic Home Video CV/AI
Wenqi Li, Mindi Ruan, Chuanbo Hu, Shuo Wang et al. · arXiv:2610.09217
Why read it
Any robot that judges a person's state from video (engagement, distress, development) faces the instability this paper measures, and its architecture is a reusable pattern for auditable social perception.
Key idea
Keep the VLM frozen and only let it write a table of timestamped, glossary-labelled behavioural events with counter-evidence; a text stage age-calibrates each row and a deterministic weight-of-evidence scorer makes the decision, so every verdict decomposes into named contributions.
Evidence
At temperature 0, across eight pipeline configurations on one backbone, 16-37% of clips change predicted label between runs. On 43 caregiver-recorded home free-play clips of preschool children, the pipeline reaches AUC 0.851 ± 0.012, 86.0% accuracy and F1 71.8 over three runs; 74.4% of clips are correct in every run (60.5% zero-shot) and no typically developing clip is flagged in every run (9 of 31 zero-shot).
Caveat
43 clips on a single backbone is a small sample; the abstract gives no information on the families' cultural or linguistic background, and it is a screening aid evaluated offline, not a diagnosis or an interactive robot setting.
RoboPace: Contact-Aware Time-Optimal Retiming for Action-Chunk Policies RO
Mimo Shirasaka, Takehiko Ohkawa, Takuya Okubo, Nicola Scianca et al. · arXiv:2610.09696
Why read it
Teams training VLAs on UMI or human-hand data inherit human timing, which is too fast in contact and too slow in free space for a robot. This is a drop-in layer with no retraining.
Key idea
Keep the policy's geometric path and only retime it online, slowing where contact is predicted and speeding up in free space within the robot's kinematic and dynamic limits.
Evidence
On three contact-rich tasks on a dual-arm robot, faster uniform execution and physical-limit-only retiming largely fail, while RoboPace achieves higher overall success than slow uniform execution and completes four of five commands in approximately half the time.
Caveat
One dual-arm platform and three tasks; the abstract does not report how accurate the contact prediction is or how retiming interacts with speed limits near people, which matters for HRI deployment.

  • SAFE: Unified Slip and Fracture Detection with Low-Cost Acoustic Sensing in Robotic Grasping RO
    Zerun Wang, Vivek Kamat, Shekhar Bhansali · arXiv:2610.08802
    Abstract

    Manipulating fragile objects remains challenging as robots must understand the state of what they grasp, such as slip or fracture, to respond appropriately, especially when material properties are unknown. In this paper, we present SAFE: a low-cost, general-purpose sensing approach that detects both slip and fracture in real time using two passive polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) acoustic sensors and motor proprioception, without relying on vision or prior material knowledge. The sensors are mounted on a compliant Fin Ray gripper, and a unified HistGradientBoosting classifier reports the state (normal, slip, or fracture) from a 79-dimensional feature vector. Under leave-one-grasp-out cross-validation, SAFE achieves an Alert-F1 of 0.884 with near-zero slip-fracture confusion, and ablations confirm that acoustic sensing is indispensable. An adaptive grasp controller built on this detection layer runs at 104 Hz on a Jetson Orin Nano, achieving 91.3% success across 46 closed-loop robot trials spanning diverse object categories, while each fixed-force strategy drops to 0% on object conditions that mismatch its preset. It further reaches 82.4% success on novel objects unseen during training, demonstrating robust, failure-aware grasp control without object-specific calibration.

  • FlashNeRD: Performance-First Contact-Rich Neural Robot Dynamics RO
    Mohammadmehdi Ataei, Mohammad Amin Nabian, Jie Xu, Miles Macklin et al. · arXiv:2610.09130
    Abstract

    Compared with analytical physics, learned dynamics models promise robot simulation that is faster, inherently differentiable, and easily adaptable to real data. Neural Robot Dynamics (NeRD) pursues this by keeping collision detection analytical and replacing a simulator's numerical dynamics for the robot with a learned model. Three limitations remain. NeRD offers little speedup over the simulator it learned from, accepts contact only at predefined points, and has no two-way coupling with objects it manipulates. FlashNeRD removes all three with a parallel streaming architecture that makes each prediction faster and more accurate, an encoder that accepts contacts wherever they occur, and two-way coupling with objects simulated by analytical solvers. Experiments across five robots show faster and more accurate dynamics, faster policy learning, and faster inference-time planning. Across three robots, FlashNeRD's dynamics model is up to $55\times$ faster than an optimized NeRD and more accurate over long rollouts. This speedup extends to policy learning, where PPO trains an ANYmal locomotion policy in 34 seconds, $3.8\times$ faster than the analytical simulator and $2.7\times$ faster than an optimized NeRD. With DIAL-MPC, a sampling-based MPC method, the robot climbs all six test platforms where fixed-contact NeRD manages one, at approximately half the analytical simulator's planning time. Cube-reorientation policies trained with FlashNeRD complete within 2% of the simulator-trained policy's target count.

  • Fast Planning for Multi-object Multi-target Throwing RO
    Zhengming Zhu, Yang Liu, Xiao Gao, Aude Billard · arXiv:2610.09224
    Abstract

    Robot throwing has emerged as a promising technique for improving efficiency in logistics and warehouse automation, by enlarging the workspace and speeding up the process. To significantly increase the throwing system's throughput, we develop strategies for throwing multiple objects in one swipe. Such multi-object multi-target throwing (MOMT) leverages the large degrees of freedom of anthropomorphic hands. The key is to quickly generate fast and feasible throwing motions, which involves a complex trade-off between short trajectory duration and short planning time. We solve this problem in two stages. Offline, we build a model of the feasible set by combining object's inverted flying dynamics and the robot's kinematics and dynamics. When target positions are randomly changed mid-execution, the system re-plans and successfully reaches the new targets within 100 ms latency without stopping the robot. These results establish the first unified planning framework for MOMT throwing -- demonstrating scalability to multiple objects in simulation and real-world feasibility on two-object tasks -- advancing the frontier of high-throughput robotic manipulation.   • RobotAPO: Adversarial Physics Preference Optimization for Robotic Manipulation Video Generation RO
    Kerui Li, Zhe Jing, Chenyi Huang, Xiaofeng Wang et al. · arXiv:2610.09454
    Abstract

    Robotic manipulation videos are increasingly used as visual plans for embodied agents, but optimizing purely for visual plausibility often fails to capture the fragile physical manifold of real-world interactions. Even minor physics-violating errors at the interaction boundary, such as interpenetration or premature object motion, can completely invalidate the inferred timing and pose needed for downstream execution. Because standard supervised fine-tuning lacks the direct pressure to penalize these localized failures, we introduce AgiBot-PhysPref. This rigorously curated 10,000-sample preference dataset isolates condition-matched physics violations, turning the generator's own failure distribution into a foundational signal for physical consistency. Building upon this, we propose RobotAPO, an adversarial physics preference optimization framework operating in the continuous flow-matching denoising space. To prevent the policy from merely memorizing static curated failures, RobotAPO employs a lightweight adversarial counterfactual proposer that learns a condition-dependent, physical-failure-biased direction in denoising space. This encourages the model to explore and better respect the physical interaction boundary, all while maintaining a pure prompt-and-reference inference interface without requiring external structural conditioning. Comprehensive evaluations demonstrate that explicitly correcting these localized physics violations improves downstream robot execution from generated videos. On held-out AgiBot conditions, RobotAPO outperforms the strongest controlled internal baseline in physical consistency by 6.8% hard score and 10.0% soft score. Crucially, in real-robot replay, it translates these physical-consistency gains into a 37.4% relative improvement in task success over the strongest controlled internal baseline.
