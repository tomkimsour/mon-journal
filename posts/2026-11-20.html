Several of today's papers find that the measuring tools themselves are wrong: benchmark success checkers, physics scores, world-model costs and age or persona prompts report things that are not true. The useful fixes diagnose where the failure is before anyone retrains a model.

Today's papers share one move: they stop trusting the number and check what produced it. Success checkers in RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365 and RoboDojo accept unfinished executions and reject valid ones. Physics-IQ rewards video models for blurring. A world-model planner's cost can be at chance on predicted outcomes while it is sound on real ones. VLMs told to answer as an older adult move valence in the direction opposite to what older people actually report. The same diagnostic habit shows up at runtime. Planners break stalls instead of adapting weights, large reasoning models are called only when a monitor flags trouble, and safety work measures what the robot physically does after it stops, not only whether the task was completed. For a team building robots for people of many ages and cultures, the most direct lesson is that human-likeness has to be checked against measured human data, item by item, and a demographic prompt does not provide that.

Insights

  1. Audit your benchmark before you trust its leaderboard. SimAudit finds both phantom successes and silent failures in three widely used simulated manipulation suites, and Kernel Physics-IQ shows a physics metric that rewards collapsed predictions. If your team reports sim success to decide what goes on the robot, feed each checker a few deliberately partial executions and a few valid but unusual ones, and treat any checker that misjudges them as a bug in your evaluation, not noise.
  2. Age and culture adaptation cannot be prompted into a model and assumed correct. The age-conditioned affect study is the clearest result today for a team building robots for people of different ages: the models shift in the direction a stereotype predicts, while real older adults shift the other way on the very images where it matters. Any persona-conditioned behaviour (tone, emotional reading, content filtering) should be scored against stimulus-level ratings from the target group, and the persona-lens results suggest checking it is unaffected by profile attributes that should not matter.
  3. Before retraining, find out where the loop breaks. Stall diagnosis, search-versus-scoring diagnosis, RoC's event-triggered agent review and Mind2Act's failure attribution all decide where the failure sits before anything is retrained. Logging that separates 'the plan stalled', 'the candidate was never tried', 'the cost ranked it wrong' and 'reasoning and execution did not compose' is cheap to add and tells you whether to spend compute on search, on the model, or on the interface between planner and policy.
  4. Measure stopping and refusal behaviour from mid-task states, with the physical outcome recorded. SAFI's finding that refusal from the initial state does not transfer to takeover partway through matters for any robot handing objects to people: a drop is a safety incident even if the forbidden task was not completed. Add mid-execution interruption tests with a held object to your safety evaluation.

Characterizing Age-Conditioned Affective Reasoning in Vision-Language Models CV
Shahriyar Zaman Ridoy, Azmine Toushik Wasi, Lin Gu, Ruogu Fang · arXiv:2611.25593
Why read it
The most direct evidence today on whether foundation models can stand in for how people of different ages perceive emotional content, which is core to robots meant to interact across age groups.
Key idea
Compare a model's change under an age prompt with the actual age differences in human ratings of the same image, rather than checking only whether the model reacts to the prompt.
Evidence
17 VLMs, 900 OASIS images, ratings from 822 participants. Without an age cue, models track average valence (median r = 0.891), but correlations between model and human age effects range from -0.030 to 0.093 against a ceiling of 0.595. On 54 sensitive pleasant images, the older-adult prompt lowers valence by 0.845 points while older participants rate them 0.443 points higher.
Caveat
It covers static image valence ratings in three age bands, not interaction, speech or culture, and the abstract does not say which cultures or countries the participants came from.
Phantom Success, Silent Failure: Auditing the Success Checkers of Simulated Manipulation Benchmarks CV/RO
Zirui Song, Jiadong Zhang, Zheng Lu, Xiaosong Ma et al. · arXiv:2611.25179
Why read it
If your team picks policies or checkpoints on RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365 or RoboDojo, some of those success numbers are wrong in a known direction.
Key idea
Use an LLM to read the instruction and the checker code, predict scenarios where they disagree, recreate them in simulation and verify the outcome visually.
Evidence
183 simulated test cases produced the predicted checker verdict, and visual verification and deduplication left 61 distinct defects across 49 tasks (40 phantom successes, 21 silent failures). RoboCasa365 errs mostly toward accepting incomplete runs, RoboDojo mostly toward rejecting valid ones.
Caveat
The abstract does not quantify how much these defects change reported policy rankings, and the auditor's own coverage (defects it misses) is not estimated.
Search or Scoring? Diagnosing Why Visual World-Model Planners Fail CV
Haoxuan Jia, Yang Liu, Shang Luo, Chenhao Wu et al. · arXiv:2611.25347
Why read it
It gives a cheap, concrete diagnostic for any team using DINO-WM or JEPA-style world models for planning, and tells you whether more search or a better model will help.
Key idea
Separate search failures (a good action was never tried) from scoring failures (the cost on predicted outcomes ranks a bad action first) by executing candidates and comparing costs on predicted and real outcomes.
Evidence
On Metaworld the cost scores real outcomes well (AUROC 0.94) but predicted outcomes at chance (0.54). Averaging retrieved demonstration windows without the model solves 127 of 168 new Metaworld tasks against at most 87 when the model chooses, and a pre-registered rule solves 678 of 960 PushT episodes against 530 for the best fixed strategy.
Caveat
Simulation only, on short tasks whose goals the planner can represent; it is not shown whether the diagnosis holds on long-horizon or real-robot tasks.
Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models to Stop Safely RO
Zheyuan Liu, Chuhan Wang, Jinduo Guo, Yue Huang et al. · arXiv:2611.26239
Why read it
It changes how to evaluate a VLA's refusal: for a robot working near people, what happens to the object in the gripper after a stop matters as much as whether the task stopped.
Key idea
Train an explicit fallback (stationary arm, consistent gripper) at intermediate states of prohibited-task demonstrations, alongside the retained tasks.
Evidence
Policies that suppress a task from its initial state can still complete it when taking over partway, and may drop held objects. With OpenVLA-OFT on LIBERO and BadLIBERO, SAFI reduces target success after takeover to 1.3% with no recorded physical incidents or action saturation, while retained-task success stays high.
Caveat
Simulation only, a single VLA backbone, and the method assumes that holding is an appropriate fallback, which may not hold when a person is pulling the object or the robot is mid-handover.
Dynamic Human-Robot Role Allocation through Entropy-Based Monitoring and LLM Reasoning RO
Elena Merlo, Marta Lagomarsino, Hamidreza Raei, Arash Ajoudani · arXiv:2611.26090
Why read it
The day's one dedicated HRI paper, and it lets the human take initiative instead of being assigned tasks, which matters for acceptance by users who want to stay in charge.
Key idea
Leave the human free to act, and allocate the robot's activities dynamically through a cost-driven behaviour tree that reads spoken preferences via an LLM and monitors hand-object interaction with Shannon entropy.

  • Learning to Move from and for Manipulation Policies: Spatializing Latent Action Representations for Goal-Pose-Free Mobile Manipulation RO
    Siyuan Zheng, Zhong Cao, Henry X. Liu · arXiv:2611.25366
    Abstract

    The success of manipulation policies is closely tied to the spatial relationship between the robot and the target object, as well as the task being performed, making the ability to adjust the robot's pose critical for successful execution. When the robot starts away from the task, such adjustments require coordinating navigation with local manipulation. Jointly training these capabilities incurs substantial computational and data costs, while navigating toward a predefined goal pose limits the flexibility needed to adapt to task-specific manipulation requirements. To address these challenges, we propose Learning to Move from and for Manipulation Policies (M4M), a framework that learns manipulation-aware mobility directly from pretrained manipulation policies. By leveraging the hidden representations of the policy's action-generating network as dense supervision, together with a limited number of manipulation execution outcomes, we learn a spatially conditioned embedding that captures task-dependent manipulation success across candidate robot poses. From the robot's current stereo observations, this representation estimates manipulation success at unvisited poses without rolling out the manipulation policy at each location. It is further integrated into navigation policy training, while a learned handoff policy decides when to transition from navigation to manipulation, enabling the robot to move and manipulate in a unified, task-aware manner. Across eight articulated-object tasks in unseen RoboCasa kitchens, M4M achieves 47.0% mean task success, compared with 26.5% for the strongest baseline and 19.2% for direct manipulation execution, and refrigerator-closing experiments show consistent improvements across four frozen manipulation policies.

  • Demonstration-Free Skill Composition for Navigation and Manipulation RO/AI
    Madeleine Nouri, Gelareh Mohammadi, Francisco Cruz · arXiv:2611.25462
    Abstract

    Hierarchical reinforcement learning enables sequential tasks to be represented as compositions of separately trained skills. This paper evaluates demonstration-free hierarchical pipelines for MiniGrid navigation and robotic manipulation, comparing their training requirements and task performance with flat reinforcement learning baselines. The evaluation concerns complete pipelines with task-specific observation, reward and control structures. In navigation, two parameterised skills are reused across unseen FourRooms layouts under manually specified high-level plans. In manipulation, reaching, grasp alignment and lifting are trained separately, with downstream skills initialised from states collected during predecessor rollouts.   • Close&Safe: Maximal Task Preservation for Redundant Manipulators via C-Space Boundary Detour RO
    Abhigyan Singh, Manthan Goyal, Fares Abu-Dakka, Ravi Prakash · arXiv:2611.26031
    Abstract

    Contact-rich manipulation such as wiping, polishing and surface inspection specifies the task as a continuous end-effector trajectory rather than a goal pose. When an obstacle absent from the planning model intersects it, conventional planners return a collision-free path that may differ from the nominal throughout, discarding trajectory that was never obstructed. We present Close&Safe (Close to the intended task, Safe from obstacles), which decouples tracking from avoidance: the nominal is followed exactly wherever it remains feasible, and avoidance acts only on the interval where it is not, so tracking is hindered as little as the obstacle allows. On that interval we sample the configuration-space obstacle boundary within a corridor around the nominal, connect the samples into a roadmap, and search it for the detour that deviates least before rejoining the nominal at the earliest feasible configuration. We evaluate across diverse simulated obstacle configurations, physical Franka experiments, and ablations of key algorithmic components. The results demonstrate collision-free trajectory repair with localized task modification, while recovering 52.4-78% in simulation and on hardware.

  • Agentic Scaling of Embodied Tasks in Simulation for Synthetic Data Collection and Policy Evaluation RO
    Kang Wang, Xiaolei Zhou, Xiaoyu Wu, Zhendong Wang · arXiv:2611.26046
    Abstract

  • UniVEP: Unifying Velocity and Endpoint Prediction for Robot Manipulation RO/CV
    Zhengtu Liang, Qiwei Liang, Tao Lin, Shihang Weng et al. · arXiv:2611.26103
    Abstract

    Flow-matching robot policies usually use one prediction target throughout action generation, even though recovering actions from noise and polishing nearly clean actions bring different challenges. We systematically compare velocity prediction (V), endpoint prediction (X), and velocity-weighted endpoint prediction (X--V) across multiple robot policies, benchmarks, and inference budgets. On StarVLA and LiLa-WAM, X--V works best for few-step generation, while V gains more from iterative refinement.   • PhysVAM: Physics-oriented Future Value Action Model for Dynamic Object Manipulation RO
    Yuxiang Yang, Jingbo Zhang, Jiaru Zhong, Junliang Chen et al. · arXiv:2611.26105
    Abstract

  • JARVIS: An Agent That Designs, Builds, and Tests Circuit Boards on Its Own RO
    Shibo Zhou, Tianqi Hong, Zheng Yan, He Yang · arXiv:2611.26503
    Abstract

    Power electronics is central to the energy transition, but developing new hardware still relies on human-led design, fabrication, testing, and iteration. We present JARVIS, an embodied AI agent that autonomously closes this loop for circuit boards. JARVIS maintains machine-specific capabilities and constraints, scans its workspace using a robot-mounted 3D scanner, and constructs an executable digital twin in which it rehearses robot motions, fabrication steps, and circuit behavior before consuming materials. A single text instruction initiates the physical experiment.   • Improving Imitation Policies for Multi-Phase Tasks through Residual Learning Allocation RO
    Clinton Enwerem, Saleh Kemal, John S. Baras, Calin Belta · arXiv:2611.25320
    Abstract

    Residual reinforcement learning corrects an imitation-trained base policy using a limited budget of environment interaction. In a task with several phases, this budget must be divided among the phases, and the phase in which the base policy fails is not necessarily the phase in which a correction most improves task completion. We formalize this distinction through the completion gain: the change in full-task completion probability when, during a single phase, a state-feedback controller replaces selected components of the base policy's action. We decompose the completion gain into the probability of reaching the phase and the conditional improvement in completion from the start of the phase, and we estimate it from paired episodes. The positive estimates determine which action components the residual policy corrects and how training episodes are distributed across phases. In stochastic point-mass docking experiments, most failures occurred in the final docking phase, whereas the largest completion gain occurred in the preceding channel phase; gain-based allocation improved completion over allocation proportional to failure counts and, in a variant with a longer approach, over uniform allocation at half of the budget. In simulated bimanual robotic assembly, the completion-gain estimates were positive only for insertion and release, although few failures occurred during release; in a second assembly configuration, a residual policy trained in the release phase improved completion from held-out states at the start of release by 16.9%.

  • MIR-Depth: Multi-Range Refinement for Single-View RGB-D Depth Completion of Transparent Objects CV/RO
    Xinzhe Fu, Qi Shao, Yuanfei Xie, Taoyu Wu et al. · arXiv:2611.25417
    Abstract

    Recovering accurate visible-surface depth from a single RGB-D image and an object mask is essential for robotic grasping and manipulation of transparent objects. Specular reflections cause missing depth, while refraction corrupts nonzero returns with background depth, making raw measurements unreliable as surface anchors. Under a fixed sampling budget, refining an initial single-view prediction within a single search interval either misses large refraction offsets or sacrifices fine local resolution. To resolve this single-view trade-off, MIR-Depth constructs Coordinate-Aware Multi-Range Hypotheses: a 3D-aggregated global inverse-depth volume and depth-axis-filtered pixel-wise local volumes around a prior-guided initial depth. Selective Recurrent Refinement then introduces out-of-bounds lookup masking and validity-masked softmax fusion to iteratively update inverse depth from these volumes using a multi-scale ConvGRU. Finally, Spatially Gated Correction applies a learned per-pixel spatial gate to the full-resolution residual, preserving accurate initial geometry without imposing raw sensor readings as hard constraints. Experiments on TransCG, TODD, TRansPose, ClearPose, and HouseCat6D show that MIR-Depth achieves lower error than ReMake, DFNet, BP-Net, and PSD.
