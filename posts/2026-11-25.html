Robot policies are being split into parts you can check: world models that predict the future without rendering it, agents that hand off to policies only inside measured boundaries, and failures traced to specific pathways and patched without retraining, while social perception starts to rest on measured cues like sound direction and gaze shifts.

Today's strongest papers stop treating a robot policy as one black box judged by average success. Six world-action models keep future prediction as a training signal but skip rendering frames at run time, bringing action latency down to 15-60 ms per step or chunk. Agent-plus-policy systems are found to fail at the handoff, and the fixes make each skill's valid start region or swept volume explicit and checkable. Diagnostics trace failures to specific causes: an instruction shortcut, reflections in a mirror, a clock that keeps running during inference. Some of these failures can be fixed without retraining. On the human side, coding agents now build and tune capabilities on a physical social robot, and perception for group interaction is being anchored in microphone direction and gaze-shift events rather than VLM guesses. For a team building robots for people of all ages and cultures, the message is to check each part on its own: when the robot asks for help, who it thinks is speaking, whether it acts within the time a person expects, and whether a skill was called from a state where it works.

Insights

  1. Treat help-seeking as a learned timing problem and evaluate it per user group. The help-request paper learns when to alert from how helpful past interventions were, and novices gain without experts losing. EmbodiSafety shows the best reference model reaches 56.1% complete decision accuracy on snapshots when it must choose between acting, clarifying and escalating to a human. RoboClock adds that the best of more than 30 methods meets time windows on 8.3% of easy tasks. For robots that serve children, older adults and expert staff, log the user's expertise next to every alert and report alert usefulness for each group, not as one average.
  2. Use cheap physical signals for who and where, and language for to whom and why. BEARING's microphone-direction prompt raises speaker accuracy by 20 points, but addressee accuracy moves only about two points, because whether someone is talking to one person or to the group depends more on the words. NOBODY shows VLMs invent people four times as often as humans do. Split the pipeline accordingly, and check that the robot's captions or logs never mention a person its sensors did not detect.
  3. Re-test world-action models on latency before ruling them out. G0.7 runs at 60 ms per 16-step chunk and FoveaWAM acts in 15.1 ms, both on one RTX 5090, and LeanWAM predicts a chunk in 25.1 ms on an A100. FoveaWAM's OnTimeBench and RoboClock both keep the scene moving while the policy computes. Adopt that protocol in your own simulation tests: a policy that is accurate but slow will miss when a person holds out an object.
  4. Gate agent-written robot code on physics, not on a kinematic check. RESET checks its reconstructed world against the demonstration before practice, EvoMisty verifies every capability on the physical robot, and the Go2 study shows that extra paper context hurt only under physics. If a coding agent writes your ROS 2 nodes or skills, require a physics simulation or hardware-in-the-loop run before merge.

Learning When a Robot Should Ask For Help: Adaptive Intervention Requests in Human-Robot Teaming RO
Yanran Jiang, Cécile Paris, Dana Kulić, Leimin Tian · arXiv:2611.31925
Why read it
This is the day's most direct HRI paper. It decides when a robot should interrupt its human supervisor, tests across user expertise levels, and includes a physical user study.
Key idea
Train a help-seeking policy with offline RL on two signals: how helpful past interventions were and how much progress the robot makes on its own. The robot then asks for help only when an intervention is likely to pay off.
Evidence
Evaluated in several task environments and at different user expertise levels in simulation, then in a physical user study. The learned policy improves exploration efficiency, reduces unnecessary alerts, and supports novice users better without compromising expert performance in real-time human-robot teaming missions.
Caveat
The abstract gives no participant count, alert rates or effect sizes. The setting is a supervisor helping with navigation and exploration, not face-to-face interaction, and the abstract does not say whether the learned timing carries over to users of other ages or cultures.
BEARING: Who Is Talking to Whom? Grounding Acoustic Direction in Vision for Online Multi-Party Conversation CV
Nader Lobandi, Lombe Chileshe, Mohammad Mehdi Hosseini, Mohammad H. Mahoor · arXiv:2611.31735
Why read it
Group conversation is the normal case for home and public robots. This paper shows that a microphone array beside the camera beats asking a VLM who is speaking.
Key idea
A label-free online calibration maps the direction a microphone array hears into the camera image. That direction then does three jobs: a visual prompt for a VLM, one input to a fusion with visual active-speaker detection, and a verifiable reward for alignment.
Evidence
On 9,498 addressee-labelled utterances from three AMI sites and egocentric EasyCom, the direction prompt raises speaker accuracy from 54% to 74%, and addressee accuracy moves by about two points. For co-located speakers on AVDIAR, visual active-speaker detection holds 95% where direction alone falls to 59%. Shrinking the lookahead from 5 s to 0.5 s costs 4.5 DER points on AMI, and the verifiable reward beats self-evaluation under GRPO, reward-ranked fine-tuning and DPO.
Caveat
It is evaluated on meeting and egocentric datasets, not on a moving robot. Addressee detection barely improves. Online calibration dips for the first two minutes. The abstract does not cover languages, accents or noisy rooms.
RESET: One Demonstration, One Verified World, Self-Improved Skills Across Embodiments RO
Zheng Sun, Zhuo Li, Zhihao Li, Hengyi Sim et al. · arXiv:2611.31894
Why read it
It turns one real demonstration into inspectable skill code that runs on several robots, with real-robot trial counts.
Key idea
One demonstration supplies three things: the scene geometry, the success criterion and a reference trajectory. RESET rebuilds the world, checks it by open-loop replay and silhouette consistency, evolves white-box task graphs from per-node failures, and deploys frozen code with no runtime LLM calls. The verified world can be shared with other robots.
Evidence
On 8 tasks, it reaches 97% average success on unseen layouts in simulation, against 27% for zero-shot code, 31% for per-task fine-tuned π0.5 and 50% for Diffusion Policy. A robot with similar arms reuses 95% of the source skill functions unchanged. On real hardware, the programs run on 4 robots and succeed in 80% of 280 trials.
Caveat
The 97% and the baseline comparison come from simulation, and the real-robot 80% is not broken down by task or robot. The authors report that geometric reconstruction errors can stall or mislead practice. Deformable and person-facing tasks are not shown.
Seeing Is Not Reaching: Instruction Shortcuts Override Visual Grounding in Vision-Language-Action Models CV/RO
Qing Zhang, Xuesong Li, Zhaoyuan Yang, Changlin Song et al. · arXiv:2611.32206
Why read it
Anyone running a VLA in a home will see it reach for where an object used to be. This paper explains the cause and gives a training-free fix.
Key idea
Separate two routes inside the VLA: an image-based grounding pathway and a direct instruction-to-action pathway. Masking each in turn shows that grounding already guides action but the instruction shortcut overrides it. Pathway-Separated Guidance strengthens the first and suppresses the second at inference.
Evidence
Across six VLAs, the authors identify a scene shortcut and an instruction shortcut. On LIBERO-PRO Object under position perturbations, PSG raises target-reaching from 22.0% to 52.6% and task success from 16.6% to 40.8%, while largely preserving performance on the original tasks.
Caveat
Results are on a simulation benchmark only, and 40.8% success is still low. PSG needs access to the model's internals, and the detailed pathway analysis is reported for π0.5.
Roboclock: A Unified Benchmark for Time-Aware Robotic Intelligence RO/AI
Yujie Zhao, Anthony L. Hu, Zimeng Huang, Zhijing Wu et al. · arXiv:2611.32233
Why read it
Around people, timing is part of being correct: an object handed over late is a failed handover. This benchmark measures that directly.
Key idea
Tasks are built around three temporal constraints (time window, reaction to an event, throughput). Evaluation is latency-aligned: environment time keeps advancing during model inference and control-interface delays.
Evidence
60 tasks across five application domains and more than 30 evaluated methods. The best-performing approach, a trained VLA, reaches only 8.3% temporal success even on easy tasks, with persistent problems in generalization and transfer.
Caveat
The abstract does not say whether the tasks are simulated or on hardware, or what compute the latencies were measured on, so absolute scores will shift with your GPU. It also does not say whether any tasks involve a human partner.

  • VG-GP: Uncertainty-Gated Visuo-Geometric Memory for Negative-Transfer-Resistant Grasp Adaptation RO
    Yonglong Zhang · arXiv:2611.31826
    Abstract

    Pretrained antipodal grasping networks generalize well to canonical shapes but frequently fail zero-shot on novel objects with irregular mass distributions, thin rims, or unfamiliar handles. While episodic experience memory adapts a frozen base policy without retraining, naive retrieval suffers from visual appearance bias, negative transfer from overriding working base grasps, and degenerate closed-loop acquisition. We present VG-GP, a plug-and-play Visuo-Geometric Gaussian Process memory framework for closed-loop $SE(2)$ residual grasp transfer. First, we fuse color-invariant surface-normal DINOv2 features, canonical $SE(2)$ gripper-aligned 3D local heightmaps, and contact-geometry descriptors under a product kernel with Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) lengthscales learned strictly on source objects. Second, we model the joint posterior over the base grasp and candidate $SE(2)$ alternatives, overriding the base policy only when the joint-covariance Lower Confidence Bound (LCB) clears a risk-sensitive threshold. Third, an online counterfactual curation gate using single-record necessity and sufficiency interventions quarantines source experiences causing negative transfer. Fourth, we close the acquisition loop via Bayesian Expected Improvement (EI) over equal-budget $SE(2)$ probes. Across 42 disjoint YCB objects in ManiSkill3 (20 source, 12 development, 10 held-out test), VG-GP wraps a pretrained GR-ConvNet base policy and raises physical lift rate across all 22 unseen objects from 38.6% (34/88) to 53.4% (47/88) with zero regressions (+13 rescues, 0 losses; object-clustered 95% CI [+6.8%, +22.7%], exact McNemar $p = 0.00024$). On the 10 held-out objects, EI active acquisition reaches 45.0% (18/40 vs. 32.5% base, 5/7 of oracle headroom), achieves 55.0% probe divergence over passive fallback, and monotonically reduces epistemic uncertainty.

  • Co-DRIS: Physics-Informed Predictive Belief and Variable-Impedance Learning for Reactive Catching under Coupled Uncertainty RO
    Yonglong Zhang · arXiv:2611.31843
    Abstract

    Reactive robotic manipulation is acutely sensitive to simultaneous sensing noise, measurement dropouts, joint execution errors, and contact parameter variations. While single-frame frameworks like Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS) achieve strong robustness under isolated uncertainties (86.4%), they degrade severely when perception and actuation uncertainties co-occur (27.6% across 10 coupled stress scenarios) due to memoryless encoding and fixed high-stiffness PD control.   • PreWAM: Exploring the Design of Latent Future Prediction for World-Action Modeling RO
    Wan Chi, Sen Li, Zihang Cui, Chuqin Huang et al. · arXiv:2611.31901
    Abstract

    World-action models combine action generation with future prediction, allowing visual transitions to serve as supervision for policy learning.   • KeyMemVLA: Subtask-Guided Key Memory for Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation RO
    Yongwei Zhang, Tao Chen, Sicheng Ma, Yuanzhe Xing et al. · arXiv:2611.32081
    Abstract

    Memory-dependent long-horizon manipulation requires vision-language-action (VLA) policies to infer task progress and recall evidence absent from current observations. Under a limited memory budget, the challenge is to retain history that resolves stage ambiguity and supports subsequent decisions. We present KeyMemVLA, a subtask-guided key memory framework based on progress indication and decision-evidence retention. A two-level annotation process selects subtasks satisfying either criterion and labels their final frames as keyframes to supervise online memory writes. A shared vision-language backbone jointly learns subtask, action, and keyframe prediction. At inference, the policy first predicts the current subtask from the instruction, visual context, and memory, then uses this semantic condition to predict actions and future memory-write positions. Actual observations are retained at the selected times during execution. We introduce KMBench to evaluate active memory retention for decisions across subtasks.
