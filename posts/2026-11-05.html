Robot evaluation is moving past one success score toward human-perceived safety, failure boundaries, and the per-hardware cost of running the policy.

Several papers today refuse to let one aggregate number stand in for a robot's behaviour. A street-perception study finds that multimodal models and 25 human respondents both shift their safety choices at night, but the models pick the humanoid over the delivery robot far less often than people do. A haptic teleoperation study shows operators drive an underwater ROV faster with cross-current cues while reporting more mental demand and frustration. Failure-boundary profiles show that driving and navigation agents with similar scores stop or push through blocked passages in different ways, and a rollout-free checkpoint ranking tracks real-robot success better than validation loss. On the data and systems side, human-video pretraining follows a log-linear trend that only pays off when the data matches the robot, and VLA inference bottlenecks change from one GPU to the next.

Insights

  1. Report human-facing measures next to task success. The persona study shows that multimodal models pick the delivery robot over the humanoid far more often than 25 human respondents do, and that a night effect for the humanoid is about three times larger in one model than in humans. The haptic ROV study shows the same split from the operator side: cues raised mental demand and frustration while operators drove faster. For a team of robots that share streets or workspaces, the next study should log perceived safety and workload from people on the same stimuli, and use models only as a cheap first screen, since the abstract says they capture only part of human judgment.
  2. Evaluate where a robot should stop, not only how often it succeeds. The failure-boundary paper finds that a driving agent that pushes through blocked corridors averages 52.2 on blocked widths, while agents that stop average 37.8 to 40.6, so a driving score rewards the wrong choice. The same method, applied to a warehouse navigation policy in Isaac Sim, shows it stops at blocked gaps in only one of three families. Add stop-versus-proceed variants to your navigation tests, and when you evaluate self-repair agents, include resampling and mismatched-feedback baselines: the self-repair study finds fixed task guidance recovers about as well as feedback from other episodes, with no detected benefit from matching the failure label.
  3. Buy human data for embodiment match, then for volume. The egocentric pretraining study reports a log-linear relation between hours and robot success, but its strongest robot numbers are in simulation, and the survey argues that the value of human data depends on kinematic, visual and contact alignment. The tactile study gives a concrete test: with 720 real-robot trials, its best single cue (marker flow) reached 74.4%, while the policy without tactile perception reached 57.8% and the full tactile setup 78.9%. Before adding a larger dataset, run a short ablation on the physical cue your task depends on, and check whether the human hand kinematics are close enough to the robot hand to transfer.
  4. Profile VLA inference on the board you ship. The inference study covers seven VLAs and four GPU families from datacenter H100 to Jetson Thor, and it finds that CPU launch overhead, memory bandwidth and kernel efficiency decide which optimization pays off. Weight compression to INT2 cut storage from 8.9 GB to 2.1 GB in one reported setting, and token routing reduced inference cost by up to 3.3 times, but the paper stresses that combinations need testing in the deployed execution mode. Measure your own latency budget on the target device first, then apply the stacking guidance in that order.

Robots in the City: Persona-Based Perceptions of Urban Robots RO
Keonhee Jang, Kanghoon Lee, Seokwoo Yun, Namwoo Kim et al. · arXiv:2611.05954
Why read it
It is the clearest direct comparison in this batch of how robot presence changes perceived safety, and it measures models against human respondents on the same question.
Key idea
Perceived street safety is measured as a pairwise choice between otherwise matched images, which lets the paper compare no-robot, delivery-robot and humanoid conditions across time of day and road class.
Evidence
18 generated Seoul street images, 500 synthetic personas per model, 25 human respondents. Both models prefer streets without robots and favour the delivery robot over the humanoid; robots are chosen more often at night and by fewer older personas.
Caveat
The images are generated rather than photographed, and the abstract reports no effect sizes or significance tests. It also covers pairwise choices only, not how people would behave around a robot.
Cognitive and Behavioural Effects of Haptic Feedback for ROV Teleoperation in Hazardous Environments RO
Joshua Brown, Ildar Farkhatdinov, Michael Jenkin · arXiv:2611.06689
Why read it
It is one of the few field-style studies of operator cognition during robot teleoperation in this set, and it reports a trade-off a deployment team has to design for.
Key idea
Haptic cues about unseen cross-currents trade mental workload for confidence: operators reported higher mental demand and frustration with the cues, but drove the ROV faster.
Evidence
An in-water ROV study with controlled cross-currents and a soft vibrating haptic touchpad. Unseen current raised physical and temporal demand; haptic cues reduced those and improved self-assessed performance.

The abstract gives no participant count and no statistics, and covers a single ROV setup in one in-water study, so the speed result should be read as a single-study signal.

Xinkai Jiang, Jing Jiang, Hongyi Zhou, Pankhuri Vanjani et al. · arXiv:2611.05667
Why read it
It turns operator takeover into a training signal for the policy, with measured gains on real manipulation tasks, which is directly usable in a teleoperation-driven data loop.
Key idea
The operator previews the policy's predicted motion in XR and intervenes before an anticipated bad action; the rejected prediction and the corrective chunk from the same physical state form a preference pair for post-training.
Evidence
Four real-world manipulation tasks. With five correction episodes per condition, success rose from 44.0% to 70.3% for a diffusion policy and from 57.8% to 76.9% for X-VLA, compared with HG-DAgger.

Five correction episodes per condition is a small correction budget, and the abstract does not say how many operators took part or how the XR interface affected their workload.

  • GRASP: Gradient-Guided Sampling for Contact-Rich Physics-Based Motion Retargeting RO
    Yang Liu, Tong Wang, Yiming Bao, Heng Yang · arXiv:2611.05754
    Abstract

    We study physics-based retargeting of human demonstrations for robot learning: finding robot controls that reproduce reference motions under contact dynamics. We propose GRASP (Gradient Recovery from Archived Samples for Proposals), which combines sampling-based exploration with gradient-guided refinement without requiring a differentiable simulator. GRASP-TO extends tempered sequential Monte Carlo by fitting local quadratic cost models to archived rollouts and using their gradients to guide Markov chain Monte Carlo rejuvenation. The refined references improve PPO tracking in pen spinning and humanoid-object interaction. We also demonstrate dexterous manipulation and whole-body humanoid motion on real hardware.

  • FWIK: Multi-Waypoint IK Selection via Lookahead Graph Search RO
    Jinglan Cao, Guohui Zhong, Chengwei Zhang · arXiv:2611.05897
    Abstract

    An ordered sequence of end-effector poses can give a redundant robot arm multiple valid joint configurations at each waypoint. Inverse kinematics (IK) computes these configurations. Choosing a configuration only for the next waypoint can leave the arm poorly placed for later waypoints. This may increase planned motion time or cause a later planning failure. This paper presents Future-Window Inverse Kinematics (FWIK), an online method that selects IK solutions by considering a small window of future waypoints. FWIK generates several IK candidates, connects them in a layered joint-space graph, and ranks candidate paths with a simple motion-time estimate. A learned model removes paths whose transitions are likely to cause self-collision. A standard MoveIt 2 planner then checks the next-waypoint IK goals from the remaining paths in ranked order, and the first goal that yields a valid trajectory is selected for execution. We evaluate FWIK in simulation on two 7-DoF manipulators, the Franka Emika Panda and the KUKA LBR iiwa. Each method-planner setting contains five repetitions of 100 sequences with 50 waypoints. Across Pilz, RRT-Connect, CHOMP, and STOMP, FWIK completes 96.8-100% of sequences and gives the shortest planned trajectory duration in all eight robot-planner settings. Its mean duration is 12.8-13.1% lower than STAMPEDE, 22.5-25.6% lower than IKLink, and 27.5-37.2% lower than retry-based TRAC-IK. The results show that a small future window can improve sequence completion and reduce planned motion time while leaving final trajectory validation to the motion planner.

  • LumiTac: Compact, High-Rate Six-Axis Optical Tactile Sensing for Human-Robot Interaction RO
    Hao Wu, Haotian Guo, Yu Feng, Yutong Wang et al. · arXiv:2611.05936
    Abstract

    Integrating six-axis tactile feedback into robotic interfaces requires compact sensors with accurate load measurement and rapid online reconstruction. This paper presents LumiTac, a low-profile, camera-free optoelectronic tactile sensor combining spatially distributed illumination with spectral encoding. Five photoemitters and five RGBW photoreceivers are mounted on opposing circuit boards coupled through translucent silicone. Load-induced interlayer motion modulates the internal light field under compression, shear, bending, and torsion. The sensing head measures 12 mm in diameter and 4.75 mm in thickness, weighs less than 2 g, and has a component cost of approximately USD 7. Experiments involving combined loading, light weights, and rapid tapping evaluated load tracking, sensitivity to small forces, and transient response, respectively. Robotic demonstrations further validated its applicability to tactile-guided manipulation and physical human-robot interaction. This study paves the way for compact, high-rate optical tactile sensing in robotic manipulation and human-robot interaction.

  • Vortex-Induced Torque in Grasping Soft Objects: Influence of Geometry and Pneumatic Parameters RO
    Roman Mykhailyshyn, Tomohiro Motoda, Ivan Virgala, Ann Majewicz Fey et al. · arXiv:2611.06359
    Abstract

    Vortex grippers are commonly integrated into automated systems for handling objects with complex geometries in packaging, sorting, and assembling. However, their interaction with compliant objects remains difficult to predict, particularly with respect to the vortex-induced torque transmitted during grasping. This study investigates vortex-induced torque during the grasping of compliant objects and quantifies the coupled effects of supply pressure, tangential nozzle diameter, surface geometry, and curvature. The results show that increasing the tangential nozzle diameter primarily enhances the sensitivity of the generated torque to supply pressure rather than producing a uniformly higher torque. These findings provide a basis for predicting and utilizing vortex-induced torque in the dexterous manipulation of soft objects.

    Abstract

  • FPTR: Feasibility-Preserving Transactional Refinement for Deadline-Constrained Joint Beam and Resource Allocation AI
    Minghao Kong, Zirong Liu, Jianhao Wu, Muyao Chen et al. · arXiv:2611.06491
    Abstract

    Short-interval wireless schedulers must return a complete, feasible beam-and-resource allocation before each execution deadline, and a late or invalid output gives no utility in the current control cycle. Searching longer can increase transmitted traffic, but it may also miss the deadline or leave only an infeasible intermediate solution. We propose Feasibility-Preserving Transactional Refinement (FPTR), which always keeps a validated feasible allocation, the incumbent. Each refinement stage builds a separate candidate and leaves the incumbent unchanged. A candidate replaces the incumbent only if it is complete, passes the feasibility validator before its stage cutoff, and strictly improves the objective; otherwise it is discarded. Four refinement stages use this rule: buffer-aware resource selection, compatibility-group (CG) user sharing, beam-mask rebuilding from remaining demand, and joint reassignment of two resources.
